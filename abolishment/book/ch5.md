# Chapter 5: Foreign Policy and the Precipice of WWIII

A third world war will not announce itself with a newsreel and a declaration on good paper. It will arrive as a regional fire that several nuclear patrons cannot afford, domestically, to be the first to put out. The title of this chapter is not a prophecy I am selling. Prophecy is for the feed. It is a structural risk: alliance transmission, energy chokepoints, nuclear opacity, a filtered public, and a stage-six empire that has confused looking strong with staying alive.

Sir John Glubb's empires ended the same way they began to end: overextended abroad while hollowing out at home. This chapter is the overseas face of the rot already located in the factory, the classroom, the file, and the ballot box. Iran and Israel are the 2026 names on that face. They are not the cause of the face. The argument is about the machine that decides which fires get called wars of necessity and which get called nobody's business, and about how that machine can walk a public down a slope that, at the bottom, has a number in its name. A public kept uninformed by Chapter 3 and unrepresented by Chapter 4 has very little say in whether its taxes and its children fund the next step.

## What a Third World War Would Actually Be

The last two world wars are a bad template and a useful one. Bad, because 2026 will not look like landing craft and factory posters. Useful, because both of those wars began as something a reasonable person could still call local, and became general through commitments that had been sold, in peacetime, as limits. A security guarantee is a limit until the week it becomes a transmission belt. An ally's emergency becomes yours. Your credibility becomes the ally's leverage. A third state's guess about what you will swallow becomes a fourth state's opening. Nobody voted for the whole. Everybody was asked to ratify a piece.

Call that the 1914 problem, updated for a world that can end a city without occupying it. A third world war, in the only sense the phrase still earns, would be a war that several nuclear-armed or nuclear-protected patrons cannot lose in public. It does not require a landing on a great-power homeland on day one. It requires a regional conflict whose patrons have already spent their domestic capital on not blinking, plus a weapons architecture in which blinking and dying can be made to look like the same act. Distance is part of the architecture. A death produced from a screen is easier to file as necessity than a death that comes home in a box. The feed will call the first week a crisis and the fifth week a new normal. New normals are how slopes hide.

Nuclear weapons are why the word III is not a marketing suffix. Glubb's empires could lose a frontier and survive as a smaller country. A nuclear exchange does not offer that courtesy. Most files on a diplomat's desk are ordinary. The precipice is not most files. The precipice is the subset in which a regional war, a nuclear-armed or nuclear-adjacent state, a web of alliances, and a domestic incentive to look strong are all present at once. Iran and Israel sit inside that subset. Naming the subset is not ranking the governments inside it. It is identifying a load-bearing risk.

Energy is the unromantic floor under the speeches. A corporate-state hybrid with a financialized energy sector and a large export market in weapons has a structural incentive to treat a chokepoint region as a permanent emergency, because permanent emergency is the condition under which risk premiums and order books both look like prudence. Strait geography, insurance markets, and the dollar's role in oil settlement are not cues for a blockade. They are why a fire in one place can reprice a life in another before the second place has had time to learn the names.

The naming machine will try to make WWIII a jersey: our war, their war, the war of the righteous, the war of the naive. Camus's measure, which occupies a later section, forbids the jersey. A third world war, if it comes, will not be a team. It will be a failure of limits. The rest of this chapter is an account of how those limits get dissolved in public, one locally reasonable step at a time.

## The Thumbnail Is Already Loaded

If you want to see the naming machine with the lighting still on, you do not have to wait for a press secretary. You can open a free video site and type the number. By the summer of 2026 a single channel, The Infographics Show, had spent years feeding a library of fifteen million subscribers a diet whose titles are the jersey in eighteen-point type. *How IRAN Will Start World War 3.* *USA vs IRAN, This Is How WORLD WAR 3 Starts.* *THIS is How Israel Struck Iran's Nuclear Sites.* *Why You Wouldn't Survive World War 3.* *What Countries Would Look Like After WW3.* *World War 3 Has Already Started. You Just Don't Know It.* *Why Everything is Going to SUCK After WW3.* The verbs do the sorting before a single map is drawn. Iran *starts*. Israel *strikes*. You *wouldn't survive*. The countries *would look*. The war *has already started*. You *just don't know it*. That last clause is the oldest trick in a sales pitch: the customer is late, the product is underway, sit down.

I am not using the channel as a general staff. It is not one. It is a factory that converts classified-sounding nouns into watch time, then converts watch time into ads, including, in at least one of the collision-course episodes, a plug for an online war game. Chapter 2's industrial system does not stop at the warehouse. It has a content wing. A twenty-minute countdown to a mushroom cloud, scored and thumbnail-ready, is a commodity. So is the comment sitting under a 2020 survival video in 2026: *this hits differently now.* *He predicted it.* *Watching this while it is about to start.* The comments are not intelligence either. They are a public walking the slope in real time, using an old episode as a liturgy, because liturgy is what you reach for when the feed has already spent your attention budget and you still need a plot.

Notice what the factory will not title. It will not title *How a Patron Walks a Public Down a Slope Without a Referendum.* It will not title *Which Deaths Arrive as Faces and Which Arrive as Estimates.* It will not title *The Integral of the Sequence.* Those are not thumbnails. A thumbnail needs a starter, a survivor, a before-and-after map. Herman and Chomsky's worthy and unworthy victims, which this chapter will take up at length, are already encoded in the playlist order. One government's move is a cinematic midnight. The other's is a button that ends the world. A third population does not get a verb. The ratio is the product. Inverting the ratio and calling the inversion justice would only prove you still work for a thumbnail.

The survival episodes are Juvenal in a basement. Bread and circuses, in a flashpoint, are not only entertainment. They are identification. You are invited to treat a distant explosion as a personal wound and a bunker list as a politics. Empathy would require the face without the jersey. Identification will take a sticker, a runtime, a subscribe button. A population raised on smart-bomb footage can be talked into regarding a sanctions line as the humane alternative and a countdown as the education. Neither is a council of the people who would have to live with the war. Both are compatible with the next shipment.

I will not recap their scenarios here as if they were briefings. Recapping them that way would complete the conversion this book exists to interrupt: faces into evidence, evidence into plot, plot into a side. The useful fact is upstream of the plot. Tens of millions of people have already been sold WWIII as a format. The format has a starter, a map, a survival tip, and a reminder to like the video. Glubb's Age of Intellect produces brilliant diagnosis. The content wing of the same age produces diagnosis you can autoplay. Stage six files both and funds the crate. If the precipice arrives, it will not be because a YouTube channel summoned it. It will be because a public trained on thumbnails was asked, at the decisive hour, to evaluate a sequence, and had been taught for years to evaluate an episode.

## The Machine That Names the Wars

A foreign policy is, among other things, a naming system. It decides which deaths will be called tragedies, which will be called collateral, which will be called justice, and which will not be called at all. The names are not decorative. They are the permission structure through which a democracy that does not in practice decide its wars is nonetheless asked to feel that it has. Once a conflict has been named a war of necessity, dissent can be recoded as naivete or as sympathy for the official enemy. Once a comparable conflict has been named nobody's business, the same dissent can be recoded as warmongering. The content of the suffering does less work in that sorting than the relationship between the sufferer and the naming machine.

Noam Chomsky's later work, particularly *Hegemony or Survival*, argued that American foreign policy since 1945 has been organized less around any coherent moral doctrine and more around the maintenance of what he called primacy: the insistence on being the deciding voice in every region that matters strategically. Under that lens, a given intervention is not explained by the stated justification of the week, whether that justification is democracy, counterterrorism, or an ally's security, so much as by whether the region in question threatens the existing arrangement of who gets to set the terms. I raise Chomsky here because the pattern he documented in Vietnam, Central America, and the Middle East of the 1980s is the same pattern a reader can trace forward into the present standoff, only with the names of the countries changed and the weapons more precise.

> If we do not want to be mere tools of power, we have to understand how it works. Rhetoric is not a reliable guide.

That sentence is the load-bearing claim of this chapter. The rhetoric will always be available. It will be fluent, it will be urgent, and it will arrive already sorted into heroes and villains a domestic audience has been prepared, by the machinery of Chapter 3, to recognize on sight. Understanding how the machine works is a different task from cheering for one of the names it has assigned.

Primacy, in Chomsky's usage, is not a synonym for safety. It is a preference ranking. A state committed to primacy will accept risks to the species, including nuclear risk, rather than accept a world in which it is merely one powerful actor among several. Survival, in the same usage, is the opposite preference: a willingness to constrain one's own freedom of action if the alternative is a chain of escalations no one fully controls. The title of the book is a fork, not a slogan. Hegemony or survival is a choice a declining empire is poorly equipped to notice it is making, because the vocabulary of security has already collapsed the two options into one word.

The collapse of those two options is not an accident of language. It is the foreign-policy version of the same trick Chapter 4 described domestically, where freedom is reserved for the ballot box and quietly withdrawn from the wage, the lease, and the hospital bill. Abroad, security is reserved for the official story and quietly withdrawn from the populations who will live under the bombs, the sanctions, and the aftermath. A public trained to hear "security" as a synonym for "our continued primacy" will not notice the substitution.

## Glubb's Sixth Stage: Overextension as Symptom

Sir John Glubb, whose six-stage cycle opened this book, treated foreign war less as a cause of decline than as a late-stage symptom of it. Stage six, decadence, is the stage in which a nation already hollowing out at home keeps the outward posture of a conqueror, because the posture is one of the last remaining proofs that the earlier ages still apply.

> The life-expectation of a great nation appears to be about two hundred and fifty years.

Glubb wrote that as a historian of agrarian and mercantile empires, not as a prophet of the nuclear age, and I do not treat the number as a law of physics. I treat it as a rhyme. It is currently 2026, the two-hundred-and-fiftieth year of the United States, which is why this book exists at all. The rhyme would be merely literary if the domestic chapters had not already described the hollowing: a financialized economy that socializes risk and privatizes profit, a labor system that converts human time into shareholder return with increasing efficiency, a classroom that teaches deference, a surveillance architecture that makes dissent expensive, and a political duopoly that filters candidates before the voter ever sees them. Stage six does not arrive as a speech. It arrives as the continuation of stages four and five after the continuation has stopped making sense.

Overextension is the foreign-policy name for that continuation. An empire in decadence does not usually reduce its commitments in proportion to its domestic capacity. It increases the theatricality of those commitments, because theatricality is cheaper, in the short run, than repair. A carrier group is more photogenic than a rebuilt water system. A sanctions package is easier to pass than a labor law that would actually shift bargaining power. A speech about deterrence is easier to write than a confession that the industrial base required to sustain the posture being deterred with has been sold off, financialized, or allowed to rust. The public of Chapter 4, its attention budget already reallocated toward spectacle, is well prepared to receive the theater and poorly prepared to audit the books.

The *form* foreign policy takes in stage six is predictably distorted: more posture, less repair; more naming of enemies, less accounting of interests; more absolute causes, fewer limited ones. Camus occupies a later section of this chapter because he understood what happens to a conscience once a cause has been allowed to become absolute. Glubb understood that empires grant themselves that permission most freely when they are already in decline and cannot bear to say so.

The battlefield, in that light, is not a departure from the factory, the classroom, the file, and the ballot box. It is those four institutions pointed outward. The factory of Chapter 2 produces the weapons and the dependence. The classroom of Chapter 3 produces the public that will not ask who profits. The file of Chapter 3, Snowden's file, produces the secret knowledge from which targeting decisions are made without a public capable of evaluating them. The ballot box of Chapter 4 produces the appearance of consent. Stage six is what you get when all four are running and the empire has run out of interior explanations for why the people inside it feel poorer, less free, and more surveilled than the speeches claim.

## Hegemony or Survival

Chomsky's *Hegemony or Survival* is often filed, by people who have not read it, as an anti-American book. That filing is itself an illustration of the naming machine. The book is a documentary argument about the internal planning record of a particular state, and about the distance between that record and the moral language produced for export. Chomsky's method, here as in *Manufacturing Consent*, is to take the government at its word in the documents it did not expect the public to read, and to measure the public word against that baseline. The result is not a conspiracy theory. It is an institutional theory. Institutions have interests. They write those interests down. They then write something else for the podium.

The planning record he traces is organized around what mid-century documents called a "Grand Area": a zone of the world, as much of it as possible, in which American corporations and American strategic preference would set the terms of trade, investment, and political development. The Grand Area was not a secret in the sense that no one had written it down. It was a secret in the sense that it was not the story told to the people whose taxes would pay for it. The story told to those people was, depending on the decade, anti-fascism, anti-communism, development, democracy, counterterrorism, or the defense of a rules-based order. Some of those stories contained real goods. Democracy is a real good. So is the prevention of mass atrocity. The propaganda model does not require that every stated justification be a lie. It requires only that the justifications which survive contact with the planning record are the ones that do not contradict primacy, and that the justifications which would constrain primacy tend not to survive.

That is a colder claim than "they lie." Lying is a vice of persons. Filtering is a vice of systems. A system can staff itself with sincere people, fund sincere scholarships, and still produce a foreign policy whose revealed preference is primacy, because sincere people who contradict primacy do not remain in the rooms where the documents are drafted. Chapter 4's club does not need a locked door. It needs dues. The foreign-policy establishment's dues are ideological as much as financial: a demonstrated comfort with the premise that the United States ought to be the deciding voice, and a demonstrated discomfort with any arrangement in which it would not be.

From that premise, preventive war becomes thinkable. Chomsky spent a considerable portion of *Hegemony or Survival* on the post-2001 revival of a doctrine that treats the mere *possibility* of a future threat as sufficient grounds for present force. The intellectual move is simple and, once licensed, almost impossible to cabin. If a state may use force to prevent a threat that has not yet materialized, then the limit on force is no longer the existence of an attack, or even of an imminent attack, but the quality of the intelligence claiming that an attack might someday occur. Chapter 3 has already described what that intelligence apparatus looks like from the inside, and how little of it the public is permitted to evaluate. A preventive doctrine plus an unevaluable intelligence system is not a security policy. It is a blank check with a flag on it.

Nuclear weapons make the check more expensive than any previous empire's. Chomsky's title is therefore not rhetorical flourish. It is a description of a new kind of decadence: an empire that can, in the effort to remain first, make the question of remaining at all a live one. The anatomy of that risk is the opening of this chapter. What *Hegemony or Survival* adds is the motive: primacy will spend survival before it will spend status.

## The Pattern with the Names Changed

I am not going to narrate the last eighty years of American war as a morality play. Morality plays require a cast of the innocent and the guilty that this chapter refuses to hire. What I am going to do is name a recurrence, because recurrences are what Glubb was counting, and because a reader who can see the recurrence is less likely to be drafted into the next naming.

Vietnam, Central America in the 1980s, the Gulf wars of the 1990s and 2000s, and the forever-war period after 2001 all share a dual ledger. The public transcript spoke of freedom, democracy, counterterrorism, or a rules-based order. The operational record, as it later surfaced, was more concerned with credibility, with clients, with precedent, and with the Grand Area's terms. The human cost was not a rounding error. It was the policy. Chomsky and Herman built the worthy/unworthy distinction on exactly this comparison: suffering attributed to official enemies arrives in high resolution; comparable or greater suffering attributable to the United States or its clients arrives as a complication, and then the feed moves on.

The forever-war period is the nearest ancestor of the present flashpoint, and it is the one a reader in 2026 is least permitted to remember clearly. A global war defined against a tactic rather than a state has no natural ending condition. A cause with no internal limit will consume the lives it was supposedly protecting, and it will do so in installments small enough that each installment can be justified on its own.

Administrations change branding. The supply chain of Chapter 4 does not. Continuity of contractors, energy firms, and revolving-door consultancies is what a stage-six empire is actually defending when it says it is defending an order. Some of the rules are real and worth defending. The order, as experienced by people who live under it rather than issue it, is the right of a particular cluster of institutions to name the exceptions.

None of this is an argument that the United States should have ignored mass murder, or that non-state actors who target civilians are redeemed by the sins of a superpower. Camus will not permit that move, and neither will I. A massacre does not become a lesser massacre because the naming machine has used it. The argument is narrower: a public that only ever receives the official enemy's massacres in high resolution, and receives the other massacres as complications, cannot do the moral arithmetic it is constantly being asked to perform. The arithmetic is being performed upstream, in the rooms Chapter 4 does not admit the voter into.

## Worthy and Unworthy Victims

Chomsky's propaganda model, introduced in Chapter 3 through *Manufacturing Consent*, was originally built to explain domestic media coverage, but he and Herman applied it just as directly to foreign policy, and it is worth returning to here at the length it deserves. Their argument was that media coverage of foreign conflicts sorts victims into two categories without ever announcing that it is doing so.

Worthy victims are those whose suffering is covered extensively, personalized, attributed to an official enemy, and used to demand a response. Their names, where they are known, are repeated. Their funerals are filmed. Their survivors are interviewed. The coverage is not a lie about the fact of the suffering. The suffering is real. The filter's work is not invention. It is amplification, and the selection of which real suffering will be allowed to structure the public's sense of what must be done.

Unworthy victims are those whose comparable or greater suffering receives a fraction of the coverage, because attributing it honestly would implicate an ally or the reporting nation itself. Their deaths, when mentioned, arrive with a cloud of caveats: the situation is complex, numbers are disputed, both sides have committed excesses, it is not our place, it happened a long time ago, it is being investigated, it is war and war is hell. War is hell. The phrase is true and, in this usage, is doing the work of a blotter, absorbing ink that would otherwise form a picture.

> The mass media serve as a system for communicating messages and symbols to the general populace. It is their function to amuse, entertain, and inform, and to inculcate individuals with the values, beliefs, and codes of behavior that will integrate them into the institutional structures of the larger society.

Herman and Chomsky were describing a filter system with five sequential screens: ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, and the dominant ideological fear of the moment. A reader of Chapter 4 already understands ownership and advertising. A reader of Chapter 3 already understands sourcing: the same official agencies that collect the secret ledger are the primary suppliers of the public one. Flak is the punitive cost of stepping outside the naming machine: the cancelled appearance, the advertiser revolt, the accusation of disloyalty. The ideological fear changes costume. It has been communism, it has been terrorism, it has been great-power rivalry, it will be whatever word is currently capable of making a question sound like a betrayal.

Applied to the current Iran-Israel standoff, the model predicts, and a fair reading of the coverage largely confirms, that the framing available to an ordinary consumer of American news is narrower than the framing available to policymakers with access to the full picture, and narrower still than the range of perspectives held by people actually living in the region. That prediction does not require me to claim that any particular casualty figure is the correct one, and I will not use this page as a scoreboard. Scoreboards are how the naming machine invites you to pick a jersey. The model's claim is about *relative* volume and *relative* simplicity. Which deaths arrive as faces. Which arrive as estimates. Which are permitted to imply a policy. Which are required to remain a tragedy without a verb.

A reader who feels a spike of anger at that paragraph is not being insulted. The spike is the filter working as designed. If you have spent years inside a media environment that has personalized one set of victims and abstracted another, a sentence that refuses to reproduce the ratio will feel like a moral failure. Camus's answer to that feeling, which I will come to, is not to invert the ratio and call the inversion justice. Inverting a filter is not the same as removing it. The anarcho-syndicalist claim running under this book is that people who do the living and the dying in a place are the people whose councils ought to structure the conversation about that place, and that a distant corporate-state hybrid is poorly positioned to appoint itself the enumerator of their dead. That claim does not require me to pretend that the governments currently claiming to speak for those people are themselves councils of the people doing the living. Most of them are not. Naming that fact about one government and forgetting it about another is, again, the filter.

I will say this as plainly as I can, because this is the section in which a certain kind of reader will decide I have taken a side. I have not. Israeli civilians are not unworthy victims. Iranian civilians are not unworthy victims. Palestinians are not unworthy victims. Lebanese families living under the flight path of somebody else's deterrence are not unworthy victims. The worthy/unworthy filter, when it runs on this conflict, does not invent Israeli death or Iranian death or anyone else's. It assigns them different narrative jobs. One set of deaths is asked to prove that force is necessary. Another set is asked to prove that force is complicated. A third set is asked to wait offstage until a later news cycle. A moral response that is worthy of Camus would refuse all three jobs. It would insist on the face. Not the jersey. The face.

## Iran and Israel as Flashpoint, Not Brief

The original table of contents put two names under this chapter: Iran and Israel. They stay because a book dated June 2026 cannot pretend the region is abstract. They stay as a flashpoint, not as a pair of desks the reader is required to staff.

A flashpoint is a structural description. It says: here is a place where existing alliance commitments, energy infrastructure, nuclear-adjacent programs, proxy networks, and domestic political incentives can combine to produce a wider war that no single actor fully intended. It does not say who should win. It does not say who started it in a way that would satisfy a partisan. Origins, in a region this long-imperialized, are a hall of mirrors, and the naming machine loves a hall of mirrors because it can always hang its portrait at the flattering end.

A few facts of position, none of them operational, all of them load-bearing for the machine rather than for a general staff. The United States has, for decades, treated Israel as a special strategic partner, with corresponding flows of aid, arms, and diplomatic cover that are not replicated for every other state in the region. The United States has, for decades, treated the Iranian government that emerged from 1979 as a primary regional adversary, with corresponding sanctions, isolation, and a history of covert and overt confrontation that did not begin in 1979 and did not begin with Iran as the only actor. In 1953, to take a date that American classrooms still underweight, American and British intelligence helped remove an Iranian prime minister and restore a monarch, a fact that does not license the present Iranian state's subsequent record and does not vanish because that record is ugly. The Islamic Republic's subsequent record is ugly: repression at home, a nuclear program that has spent years in the gray zone of breakout anxiety, and a network of regional clients whose methods include the targeting of civilians. Israel's subsequent record includes the occupation of Palestinian territory, repeated campaigns whose civilian cost is not a rumor, and a nuclear arsenal that is treated, in American public discussion, as a fact too delicate to name with the same vocabulary applied to Iranian enrichment. Holding all of those sentences at once is the minimum adult position. The naming machine is built to make holding them at once feel like treachery.

I am not going to adjudicate a peace plan on this page. I am not qualified to, and more importantly, a writer sitting inside the empire that has spent seventy years treating the region as a Grand Area subsidiary is not the person whose qualification would matter. The people who live there are. That sentence will be read by some as a dodge. It is the opposite. It is the anarcho-syndicalist premise applied where it is least fashionable: the people who do the producing, the surviving, and the burying ought to federate their own arrangements, and a distant shareholder-state has no proprietary right to their future. Whether those people currently have institutions that resemble workers' councils is a separate and mostly tragic question. Most of them do not. Exporting a new set of masters, or blessing the existing ones with more explosive inventory, does not bring those councils into being.

What the American public is offered instead is a binary. One is asked, in effect, to become a brief for Jerusalem or a brief for Tehran, as if those were the only two desks on earth, as if Palestinians were a subplot, as if Yemeni and Lebanese and Syrian and Iraqi civilians were extras, as if the Iranian street that has repeatedly risked its own safety against its own government were identical with that government, as if Israeli dissenters who do not want their children used as a blunt instrument of a regional forever-war were identical with the most maximal cabinet available in any given year. Binaries are convenient for Chapter 4's attention market. They are convenient for fundraising. They are convenient for the arms manufacturers who can sell into a binary more easily than into a ceasefire. They are not a description of the region.

The nuclear file is the binary's favorite instrument, and it deserves a paragraph that does not pretend to classified knowledge I do not have and would not publish if I did. A world of nuclear weapons is a world in which some states are permitted the weapon, some are permitted ambiguity, and some are told that even the capacity to approach the weapon is a hanging offense. The distribution of those permissions tracks the Grand Area more closely than it tracks a consistent theory of human survival. That is not a defense of proliferation. Proliferation makes Camus's precipice more crowded. It is a defense of noticing that a nonproliferation regime which is applied as law to official enemies and as etiquette to official allies will not be experienced, by the people subject to it, as law. It will be experienced as hierarchy. Hierarchy, as Chapter 1 argued through the Tea Act and the East India Company, is exactly the arrangement that produces the kind of revolt the hierarchy then cites as evidence that more hierarchy is required.

Sanctions belong in the same paragraph as bombs even though they photograph less well. A sanctions regime is an act of force. It is a slower act of force, which is why a public raised on smart-bomb footage can be talked into regarding it as a humane alternative. Peter Joseph's structural violence is the right analytic here. Scarcity in the midst of a world of plenty, imposed as policy, distributes its harm downward. Elites find medicine. Children find empty pharmacies. A government targeted by sanctions can often convert the resulting misery into domestic legitimacy, at least for a time, by pointing at the empire that imposed them. This does not make the targeted government innocent of its own cruelties. It makes the humanitarian language that sometimes accompanies sanctions look, to the people standing in the pharmacy line, like the naming machine at work.

Strait geography, energy prices, insurance markets, and the dollar's role in oil settlement are the unromantic infrastructure under the speeches. I mention them because Chapter 2's industrial system still runs through them, and because a foreign policy that cannot admit its material interests will lie about its moral ones. I will not translate those interests into a recommendation. A recommendation is a brief. A description is not. The description is that a corporate-state hybrid with a financialized energy sector and a large export market in weapons has a structural incentive to treat the region as a permanent emergency, because permanent emergency is the condition under which both the energy sector's risk premiums and the weapons sector's order books look like prudence rather than like business.

Proxy networks complicate every simple map. States in the region act through and against non-state militias, parties, and movements, some of which have genuine social bases and some of which are little more than subcontractors of violence. The United States does this too, and has done it for decades, which is a fact of the operational record rather than a talking point. A public trained to see only the other side's proxies as illegitimate will not recognize its own country's fingerprints when they appear in the same style of work. I will not catalog those fingerprints here in a way that would be useful to a participant. I will say that a flashpoint is, almost by definition, a place where the proxies and the patrons can pull one another past a line that none of them can easily walk back from. That is the precipice. Not the identity of any one patron. The interaction.

And then there are the people, who keep being turned into evidence. The Israeli family in a safe room is not a talking point. The Iranian family in a blackout is not a talking point. The Palestinian family picking through a building that was a building yesterday is not a talking point. Camus's entire effort in *The Rebel* was to stop the conversion of faces into evidence for an absolute cause. If this chapter does nothing else, it should fail to complete that conversion. The machine will complete it whether I help or not. I am not required to help.

## The Rebel and the Absolute Cause

Albert Camus offers something Chomsky's structural analysis does not: a way to think about the individual conscience inside a system built on sanctioned violence. In *The Rebel*, Camus draws a line between rebellion and the kind of ideological violence that justifies any cost in the name of a future good it can never actually guarantee. His worry was that once a cause becomes absolute, the people sacrificed for it become abstractions, statistics rather than deaths.

> What is a rebel? A man who says no, but whose refusal does not imply a renunciation. He is also a man who says yes, from the moment he makes his first gesture of rebellion.

The yes matters as much as the no. Camus's rebel refuses a particular humiliation, a particular lie, a particular order to treat a person as a thing, and in that refusal affirms a limit: there is a human measure that the order is not permitted to cross. The revolutionary of Camus's warning, by contrast, says no to the entire existing world in the name of a future one, and because the future one does not yet exist, there is no present measure against which the costs can be checked. Anything can be done to the people of now for the sake of the people of later. Later never arrives in a form that can collect the debt.

The structure he named is portable. An empire can make security sacred in the same way a party can make history sacred. A theocracy can make God sacred in the same way. A nationalist cabinet can make a particular soil sacred in the same way. Once the cause is sacred, the person is not. The precipice in this chapter's title is the moment at which several sacred causes, each internally justified, occupy the same geography and discover that they cannot all be absolute at once.

Camus's measure, if it can be stated without turning it into a slogan, is that a rebellion which destroys the human being it meant to defend has ceased to be a rebellion and has become an office of the thing it opposed. Applied to this chapter, the measure forbids several cheap positions at once. It forbids the position that Iranian state violence is redeemed by American hypocrisy. It forbids the position that Israeli state violence is redeemed by Iranian hypocrisy. It forbids the position that American state violence is redeemed by either. It forbids the position that a civilian death on one side of a border is a tragedy and a civilian death on the other side is a lesson. It forbids, also, the position that a writer who refuses to brief a war is therefore asking the people under the war to lie down. Refusal to become an office of the machine is not the same as indifference to the people the machine is using.

> In order to exist, man must rebel, but rebellion must respect the limits that it discovers in itself.

Those limits are the opposite of decadence as Glubb described it. Decadence, in the sixth stage, is the exhaustion of limits: a society that can explain everything and restrain nothing, that can diagnose its own cruelty in elegant essays and then fund the next shipment because the shipment has already been named necessary. The Age of Intellect, stage five, is the stage that produces the elegant essays. Stage six is the stage that files them.

The limit is not a personal ethic only, a way to keep clean hands while the shipments continue. It is a political claim about what kinds of organizations are fit to wield force at all. An organization that cannot name a stopping point before it starts is not fit. A state that has collapsed hegemony and survival into a single word is not fit. A movement that has decided its woundedness is a permanent license is not fit. Fitness, here, is not a mystical quality. It is the capacity to treat the person in front of you as a limit rather than as fuel.

The anarcho-syndicalist thread of this book meets Camus at exactly that sentence. Workers' councils, federated production, the dissolution of corporate rule: these are institutional ways of keeping the human measure close to the decision. A council of the people who would have to live with a war is a different deliberative body from a committee of the people who would have to live with a dip in a contractor's quarterly earnings. I do not claim that councils are incapable of cruelty. Human beings are capable of cruelty in any arrangement. I claim that an arrangement which systematically distances the decider from the consequence, which is the arrangement Chapters 2 through 4 have been describing, will produce cruelty as an output even when the deciders are, in their own telling, the grown-ups in the room.

## The Slope, Not the Cliff

A nation standing at the edge of a wider war is rarely pushed by a single decision. It is walked there by a long accumulation of smaller ones, each justified in the moment, each treating the next escalation as regrettable but necessary, until the absolute cause has quietly consumed the individual lives it was supposedly protecting.

The precipice is not a cliff a nation falls off in a single dramatic moment. It is a slope. The slope is harder to photograph than the cliff, which is why the naming machine prefers the language of turning points, red lines, and last chances. A turning point can be sold. A slope can only be described, and descriptions do not raise the same kind of money.

Each individual step (a sanctions package, an intelligence-sharing agreement, an arms shipment, a strike described as targeted, a resolution, a naval transit, a further resolution) arrives with its own self-contained justification, evaluated in isolation from the steps before it and the steps it makes more likely afterward. Isolation is the trick. If you evaluate a sanctions package only as a sanctions package, you can have a reasonable-sounding argument about leverage. If you evaluate an arms shipment only as an arms shipment, you can have a reasonable-sounding argument about deterrence. If you evaluate a targeted strike only as a targeted strike, you can have a reasonable-sounding argument about precision. The slope does not live in any one of those arguments. It lives in the series. Camus's point was that a person or a state committed to an absolute good, whether that good is called security, deterrence, or liberation, will eventually justify almost any cost in that good's name, because the cause has stopped being weighed against competing values and started being treated as the only value that counts.

I want to be painstaking about what this paragraph is not. It is not a list of tools a reader should go use. It is not a sequence to accelerate or to invert as a strategy. It is a description of how modern conflict escalation is actually sold to the public, because the sale is the part the public is permitted to see. The operational sequence is compiled in rooms the public does not enter, on the basis of signals intelligence the public is not shown. A citizen who can be made to ratify each step as if it were a standalone essay question can be walked down a slope they would have refused if it had been presented as a single question: are you prepared to risk a regional war that could draw in nuclear-armed patrons for the sake of a primacy you were never asked to vote on?

Let S denote a sequence of escalatory steps and let J(s) denote the public justification attached to a given step s. The propaganda model does not require that J(s) be false in every particular. It requires that J(s) be *locally* sufficient (sufficient to pass, this week, in this news cycle) and that the public never be asked to evaluate J(S), the justification of the sequence as a whole. Call that missing evaluation the integral of the slope. A public that cannot see the integral can be made to walk it. Chapter 3's education system is not designed to teach integration of this kind. Chapter 4's attention market is designed to interrupt it. The feed will always have a new local J(s) ready before the last one has been checked against the deaths it already cost.

Alliance structures make the integral harder, not easier. A security commitment that was originally sold as a limit on an adversary's behavior becomes, in a crisis, a transmission belt. The ally's emergency becomes yours; your credibility becomes the ally's leverage; a third state's calculation of what you will swallow becomes a fourth state's opportunity. I am describing a known property of alliance systems in a world of nuclear weapons, not recommending a betrayal and not recommending a blank check. Both of those recommendations would be briefs. The description is that a stage-six empire, anxious about its status, is especially likely to confuse credibility with primacy, and to treat every local challenge as a test of the whole. Tests of the whole are how slopes become cliffs. Glubb's decadent empire is not uniquely evil in this. It is uniquely unable to tell the difference between a local interest and an existential one, because the interior sources of meaning have thinned, and the exterior posture is doing the work that meaning used to do.

Domestic incentives steepen the slope. A politician filtered by Chapter 4's donor screen does not need to be a monster to prefer the appearance of toughness over the appearance of restraint. Toughness photographs. Restraint looks, in a feed optimized for outrage, like weakness, and weakness is the one unforgivable brand defect in a duopoly that competes on branding while agreeing on supply. The arms sector does not need to buy a war outright. It needs only to fund the ecosystem of certainty: the think tanks that always conclude that more inventory is required, the retired officials who always find the current inventory insufficient, the local employment maps that make a particular weapons plant look like a town's last remaining factory, which, given Chapter 2, it sometimes is.

The slope, then, is not walked by a single villain. It is walked by a public whose information is filtered, whose attention is reallocated, whose labor has been stripped of leverage, whose votes are collected after the candidates have been screened, and whose conscience has been offered an absolute cause as a substitute for a politics that would actually reach the rooms where the sequence is compiled. Iran and Israel are the present names on the slope. They will not be the last names unless the interior arrangement that produces the slope is altered. Altering that arrangement is the business of the conclusion of this book, and it is not a military business.

## Signals Intelligence and the Unseen Ledger

Snowden's revelations add a second layer to this. The same surveillance infrastructure described in Chapter 3 does not stop at domestic borders. Signals intelligence collected under programs he exposed has long been used to inform targeting decisions abroad, including the drone campaigns that expanded significantly in the decade before his disclosures. The public debate about those campaigns, when it existed at all, took place almost entirely after the fact and almost entirely without the information necessary to evaluate whether the stated justification matched the operational reality, the exact gap between story and practice that radicalized Snowden in the first place.

> I don't want to live in a world where everything that I say, everything I do, everyone I talk to, every expression of creativity or love or friendship is recorded.

He was talking, in that sentence, about a domestic life under bulk collection. The foreign application of the same collection is the part a patriot of the conventional type is trained not to mind. Foreigners, the training says, do not have the same claims. The training is convenient for a Grand Area. It is also, as Snowden documented, a legal fiction even on its own terms, because the architecture of global communications does not respect the civics textbook's map. A system built to hoover the outside will take the inside with it. A system built to hoover the inside will not refuse the outside. The fiber does not know which chapter of this book it is in.

A citizenry that cannot access the information behind a domestic surveillance program is even less likely to access the information behind a foreign targeting decision, and a media environment sorted by worthy and unworthy victims is poorly equipped to demand that information even when it exists. This is not a request for the publication of methods, identities, or anything a reasonable person would recognize as a gift to a participant in a war. It is a request that the public notice the *shape* of the epistemic situation it is in. The situation is that the most consequential facts about a flashpoint (what was intercepted, how it was interpreted, which uncertainties were dropped from the briefing, which allied intelligence services had an incentive to drop them) are precisely the facts the public is not permitted to have. The public is then invited to have a vigorous debate inside the remaining spectrum. Chapter 3 already described this as the genius of the propaganda model: lively argument, narrow bounds.

Snowden's own trajectory remains the most useful illustration because it is not a story about a man who began as an ideologue. He describes himself, before the years inside, as a conventional patriot, a believer in the sanctioned story. What changed him was proximity. He watched the distance between what the public had been told and what was being built, and the distance was the radicalizing agent. Apply that observation to a flashpoint. The public is being asked to ratify a slope on the basis of a story. The operational ledger is being compiled from intercepts, from allied sharing, from analyses that will not be shown, from error rates that will not be advertised. Every large collection system has error rates. A targeting process that runs on a collection system inherits them. A public that is only shown the successful justifications will imagine the error rate is zero. Imagining the error rate is zero is how a democracy consents to a machine it cannot see.

The sharing arrangements Snowden documented mean that the ledger is not even purely national. It is federated among states, which would be an irony worth smiling at in a book about federated production if the federation in question were accountable to the people being collected rather than to the clubs doing the collecting. It is not. A union of intelligence services is the opposite of a union of workers. It federates the watchers. The watched remain as atomized as Chapter 2's gig worker, as self-policing as Chapter 3's panoptic subject, as electorally decorative as Chapter 4's voter.

I will not pretend that a modern state can conduct foreign policy with no secrets at all. The question is who the secrets are *for*, and whether a secret that structures a war can be permitted to sit entirely outside the people who will pay for the war with money and with sons and daughters. A secret which cannot in principle be audited by anyone the public can identify is not a secret in the old, limited, professional sense. It is a second government. Chapter 4's big club already described the first extra-electoral government, the one that runs on money. The intelligence club is the one that runs on classification. Together they are quite enough to walk a slope without a referendum. The distance between picture and story is not an emergency measure from a week of crisis. It is the default setting. Default settings, as Chapter 3 argued, are more important than scandals. Scandals can be survived. Default settings are the thing the scandal briefly illuminated and then left running.

## The Arms Economy and the Club Abroad

Chapter 2 described an economy that converts human time into shareholder return as efficiently as possible. Chapter 4 described a political duopoly whose supply chain is campaign finance. This section is only those two chapters with a passport and a procurement code.

A weapons export is not a foreign policy in the civics-textbook sense. It is a sale. It is also a policy, because the sale structures the possible futures of the people who live at the other end of it. A country whose industrial policy has been allowed to atrophy everywhere except in a handful of sectors will defend those sectors with the intensity of a stage-six empire defending its last proofs of competence. They employ real people who are not villains. They also constitute a lobby whose revealed preference is that the world remain at least as dangerous as last year's annual report required.

I am not going to publish a shopping list, a ranking of systems, or anything that would help a purchaser. The critique is upstream of the catalog. It is that a corporate-state hybrid which has already learned, domestically, to socialize risk and privatize profit will do the same with war. The profit from the shipment is private. The risk of the slope is socialized onto volunteers, onto distant civilians, and onto a public that will be handed the reconstruction invoices as if they were natural disasters. Chomsky's primacy is, among other things, a business climate. Stability of a certain kind (the kind in which contracts are honored and clients remain clients) is profitable. Stability of another kind (the kind in which regional populations set their own terms) is less so. The naming machine will call the first kind peace and the second kind chaos. Peter Joseph would call the difference structural.

Iran's position adjacent to a major energy passage is a fact about the machine, not a cue for a blockade recommendation. Israel's position as a partner in a U.S.-aligned security architecture is a fact about the machine, not a cue for a blank check. The flashpoint is where those facts sit next to a nuclear-adjacent conflict and a set of patrons who cannot afford, domestically, to look as if they were the first to blink. Looking as if you were the first to blink is, in a decadent empire, a more immediately costly event than a distant civilian death. That comparison is obscene. It is also, as a description of incentives, accurate. Camus's measure exists to call the obscenity by its name rather than to ratify the incentive.

The club abroad is the same club as Chapter 4's, with better hotels. It is the donor class, the contractor class, the revolving-door class, the intelligence-contractor class Snowden worked inside, and the media-ownership class that runs the worthy/unworthy filter. It does not meet as a single committee. Continuity of interest is a stronger organizing principle than continuity of personnel. The dues are paid in the form of a demonstrated comfort with primacy, a demonstrated comfort with classification, and a demonstrated discomfort with the claim that the people who do the work and the dying ought to set the terms.

## A Public Without a Lever

The empire's foreign wars, like its industrial economy, are decided upstream of the voter, and the closer a nation gets to the sixth stage of Glubb's cycle, the more those upstream decisions tend to be made in the name of security and paid for in the coin of decline.

Chapter 4 already said that a citizen who votes every two or four years for one of two donor-vetted candidates has control mostly in the form of a complaint mechanism rather than an actual lever. Foreign policy is the domain in which this is most obviously true, because it is the domain most thoroughly ringed by classification, expertise-as-credential, and the accusation that the uncredentialed are children. Some of the expertise is real. The abuse is not the existence of expertise. The abuse is the conversion of expertise into a license to exclude the people who will bear the costs from the deliberation about the costs. Anarcho-syndicalism, as this book uses it, is among other things a claim about whose expertise counts. The warehouse floor knows things too. The family in the safe room knows things. The nurse in the blackout knows things. A council that cannot hear them is not a council. It is a club.

Malcolm X's argument in "The Ballot or the Bullet," which Chapter 4 carried domestically, generalizes here with almost no translation. A community that can be assumed will be taken for granted. A public that can be counted on to rally around the flag for any named emergency will be supplied with named emergencies. This is not because a hidden committee is inventing emergencies out of nothing. It is because a system that needs primacy, a weapons sector, a filtered public, and a slope it can walk without a referendum will not have to invent from nothing. The world supplies raw material. The naming machine supplies the cut.

Juvenal's bread and circuses, also from Chapter 4, have a wartime mode, and the mode now has a production schedule. Spectacle during a flashpoint is not only entertainment. It is identification. One is invited to wear a color, to treat a feed as a front, to experience a distant explosion as a personal victory or a personal wound. The Infographics Show did not invent that invitation. It industrialized it: a new WWIII title whenever the map runs hot, a survival episode whenever the comments ask for a bunker, a before-and-after of the planet as if the planet were a skin you could swap. Identification is not empathy. Empathy would require the face without the jersey. Identification requires the jersey and will accept a sticker in place of the face. A population whose attention budget is already exhausted by the peacetime feed will not suddenly acquire a surplus of careful attention when the feed switches to war colors. It will acquire a surplus of certainty. Certainty is cheap to produce and expensive to undo.

I do not say the public is stupid. I have spent three chapters arguing that the public is *worked on*: educated into deference, watched into self-censorship, entertained into exhaustion, employed into precarity. A people in that condition can still think. They do think. They are not permitted to *connect* their thinking to a mechanism that would change the sequence. The disconnection is the point. A connected public would be a council in embryo. Chapter 3's panopticon and Chapter 4's duopoly exist, in part, to prevent the embryo from recognizing itself.

So the lever that remains is the one this book has been describing since the Author's Note, and it is not a rifle and not a ballot in isolation. It is organization among the people who already do the work: the refusal, peaceful and stubborn, to treat corporate rule as weather; the construction of federated bodies that can bargain, produce, and deliberate without waiting for the club's permission; the withdrawal of legitimacy from a naming machine that cannot tell a face from a jersey. That is not a foreign policy. It is the interior condition without which a humane foreign policy will not be permitted to exist. A stage-six empire will always find a flashpoint. The question is whether the people inside the empire have any institution left that can say no to the slope without being named as the enemy of the absolute cause.

## Where the Mirror Points

That is where this book's argument comes full circle. An empire in Glubb's Age of Decadence does not usually recognize the stage it is in while it is happening. It recognizes affluence, it recognizes intellect, it recognizes its own historical greatness in the mirror it has built for itself, and it mistakes the accumulation of all three for permanence. The chapters before this one traced how that mirror gets built, in the factory, the classroom, the surveillance file, and the ballot box. This chapter traces where the mirror eventually points: outward, toward a conflict abroad, at the exact moment a public equipped to notice the pattern at home has the least capacity left to stop its repetition overseas.

Iran and Israel are the names on the mirror in 2026. They are not the cause of the mirror. A reader who finishes this chapter as a partisan of one of those names, and only that, has been recaptured by the device the chapter was written to describe. A reader who finishes it as a partisan of American primacy has been recaptured just as thoroughly, only in the original language of the Grand Area. A reader who finishes it as a partisan of somebody else's primacy has inverted the jersey and kept the game. Camus's rebel would finish it as a partisan of the limit: the human being who is not fuel.

This chapter refuses to brief a war. It refuses to treat a flashpoint as a puzzle for clever people in a distant capital to solve with other people's children. It refuses to pretend that the corporate-state hybrid described in this book is a neutral referee of the world's suffering. It refuses, equally, to treat that hybrid's official enemies as automatically virtuous, because automatic virtue is just the worthy/unworthy filter with the polarities flipped. It affirms that the people who live in the blast radius, in the sanctions line, in the safe room, and in the warehouse that made the crate are the people whose councils ought to matter. It affirms that a public which cannot see the operational ledger can still see the slope, if it is shown the series rather than the episode. It affirms that 250 years is long enough for a nation to learn that the ruins Paglia mentioned were not produced by a lack of speeches.

> The Earth is littered with the ruins of empires that once believed they were eternal.

The precipice of WWIII is a risk inherent in a system that has confused primacy with survival, classification with safety, jerseys with faces, and decadence with destiny. Chomsky mapped the confusion. Herman helped him name the filter that keeps the map off the evening news. Snowden showed that the map's most consequential quadrant is classified. Glubb showed that the confusion arrives on a schedule that rhymes with the calendar we are now living inside. Camus showed what a person is required to refuse even when the calendar is ringing.

The next pages are the conclusion, and they are where this critique has to become an alternative, or else it is only stage-five intellect: brilliant, true enough, and compatible with the next shipment. The alternative will not be a war plan. It will be the same claim the Author's Note started with, now earned by the five chapters that followed. Abolishment, in this book, means the dissolution of corporate rule over daily life, a peaceful transfer of power back to the people who already do the work, organized in councils and federated by consent rather than owned downward by shareholders. We The People is not a costume for a rally. It is the only adult answer to a machine that has learned to walk a slope in our name.
