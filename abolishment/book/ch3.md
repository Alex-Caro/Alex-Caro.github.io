# Chapter 3: The Illusion of Education and Surveillance Capitalism

Every empire needs its citizens to believe two things at once: that they are free, and that they are watched for their own good. This chapter is about the machinery that produces both beliefs simultaneously, and about the two men on my list of influences who spent their careers documenting it from opposite sides of the desk. I want to be precise about what I mean by machinery, because the word can sound like conspiracy when it is only architecture. There is no single room in which a committee decides that children will be taught a sanitized civics while their parents' location histories are sold to a federal contractor. There is a set of institutions that reward those outcomes, starve the alternatives, and then congratulate themselves for remaining within the law. The law, in this arrangement, is not a constraint so much as a costume.

I write this in June of 2026, a month before the republic's two hundred and fiftieth birthday, which is a convenient moment for a country to congratulate itself on the story it tells in classrooms. The story is that a free people, educated in the habits of self-government, keep watch over their institutions. The operational reality, which this chapter will spend its length documenting, is closer to the reverse: the institutions keep watch over the people, and the education system trains the people to experience that watching as protection. I am not claiming that every teacher is a propagandist or that every police officer is a villain. I am claiming that a system can be staffed by decent people and still produce, as its ordinary output, a public that has been taught to confuse the appearance of civic knowledge with the practice of civic power.

> A student can be taught to think critically about everything except the institutions doing the teaching.

That sentence is the load-bearing beam of what follows. The rest of the chapter is an attempt to show how the beam was installed, who benefits from the building that rests on it, and why the surveillance apparatus described later in these pages so rarely provokes the outrage its scale would seem to demand.

## Two Beliefs at Once

Start with the contradiction, because once you see it you will have trouble unseeing it. A free person, in the civic vocabulary this country prefers, is a person who may speak, assemble, petition, and move without having to account for those acts to a sovereign. A watched person, in any vocabulary that has not been laundered through a press office, is a person whose speech, assembly, petition, and movement are recorded, stored, and available to be reconstructed later by an authority the person did not choose and cannot inspect. These two conditions cannot occupy the same body at the same time except by a trick of language. The trick is to rename the watching. Call it safety. Call it national security. Call it a terms-of-service agreement the user clicked because the alternative was to live outside the networks that now constitute ordinary life. Call it, in the schoolhouse, preparation for citizenship.

I do not think most of the people who administer this trick are lying in the theatrical sense. A teacher who assigns a chapter on the Bill of Rights is not, in that hour, conspiring with a data broker. A local police department that accepts a federal information-sharing agreement is not, in the hour of the signing, rewriting the Fourth Amendment. The trick works precisely because no single actor has to hold the whole of it in mind. Education produces the belief in freedom. Surveillance produces the fact of being watched. The two operations run on different budgets, in different buildings, under different letterheads, and they meet only in the citizen, who has been trained to experience their coincidence as the normal condition of a modern democracy.

This is why I have paired them in a single chapter rather than treating the classroom and the camera as separate subjects. They are not separate. An education system that teaches deference produces a public less likely to ask what the surveillance is for. A surveillance system that never has to justify itself produces a public less likely to notice what it was never taught to question. Each half of the arrangement makes the other half cheaper to maintain. That is not a metaphor. It is an accounting identity, and later in this chapter I will write it the way I wrote the complexity-and-autonomy relation in Chapter 2, because some arguments become harder to dismiss once they have been forced into the shape of a function.

The pairing also explains a feeling I expect many readers already have and have not been given a name for. You can sit through twelve years of schooling in which the word freedom appears on banners, in recitations, and in the closing paragraph of every unit on the founding, and still leave that schooling unable to describe, in any operational detail, how a National Security Agency collection program works, how a fusion center shares data with a county sheriff, or how a commercial data broker can assemble a file on a person who has never been charged with a crime. That gap is not an accident of crowded curricula. It is the point at which the two beliefs are installed as a pair: you are free, and the watching is for people who are not you.

## Manufacturing Consent Before the First Bell

Noam Chomsky, writing with Edward Herman in *Manufacturing Consent*, described a propaganda model in which the news and the classroom do not need an overt censor to produce a compliant public. The filtering happens upstream, in what gets funded, what gets published, and which experts get invited onto the panel. I bring Chomsky in here specifically because his model explains why the surveillance apparatus described below rarely provokes the outrage its scale would seem to demand: the public has been pre-sorted into believing the watchers are the good guys, by an education system funded by the same interests the watchers protect.

The model is worth unpacking without the academic frost that sometimes accumulates on it, because its five filters are not abstractions. They are decisions a person can watch being made, if the person has been taught to look. The first filter is the ownership of the media itself, the fact that the major channels through which a public learns what is happening are firms with owners, boards, and advertisers, which is to say firms with interests that are not identical to the public's. The second is advertising as the primary income source, which means that a story which disturbs an advertiser does not have to be banned; it only has to become slightly more expensive to run than a story that does not. The third is the sourcing of news, the gravitational pull toward official statements, official experts, and official frames, because those are the cheapest and safest inputs a newsroom can process under deadline. The fourth is flak, the organized punishment that arrives when a story strays too far from the sanctioned range, not always as a lawsuit, often as a campaign of letters, a threatened boycott, a career consequence. The fifth, in the original formulation, was the ideology of anti-communism, later updated by Chomsky and others as a more general anti-other, a designated enemy against which almost any domestic arrangement can be made to look like a lesser evil.

I am less interested, in this chapter, in litigating the model's application to any single newspaper than I am in noticing that the same filters operate, with only cosmetic changes, on the materials a child encounters before that child is old enough to buy a newspaper. Textbook adoption is an ownership-and-funding problem. Standardized testing is an advertising-and-market problem, in the sense that the firms which write the tests also write the prep materials and also, in more than a few states, advise the departments of education that adopt them. The sourcing of civic knowledge is overwhelmingly official: the textbook's account of the FBI, the police, and the intelligence community is the account those institutions prefer, supplemented by a paragraph of acknowledged excess if the excess is old enough to be described in the past tense. Flak arrives when a teacher assigns a book that treats COINTELPRO as a central fact rather than a footnote, or when a district considers a curriculum that would ask students to audit a local police budget the way they are asked to audit a Shakespeare sonnet. The designated enemy changes with the decade (communism, terrorism, drugs, gangs, cartels, "disinformation") and in each case it performs the same service. It makes the watching look like a response rather than a structure.

> The filtering happens upstream, in what gets funded, what gets published, and which experts get invited onto the panel.

A public that has not been trained to notice filters will experience filtered news as the world. A public that has been trained, from the first pledge of allegiance, to associate the flag with protection and the badge with neutrality will not, as a statistical matter, become a public that demands to see the warrant. This is the sense in which education is the installation of consent rather than its opposite. Students learn a great deal. What they learn has been routed through a set of gates that are almost never themselves the subject of the lesson.

## The Filters That Never Announce Themselves

It is useful to watch a filter operate in a setting that does not look like propaganda, because the settings that look like propaganda are the ones a civics course has already taught a student to discount. Consider the ordinary American history survey as it is still taught in the majority of public high schools. The course has a shape. It begins with exploration and settlement, moves through revolution and constitution, spends a respectful interval on the Civil War, accelerates through industrialization and the world wars, and arrives at the present with a unit that is always too short and always framed as current events rather than as history the student is living inside. Slavery is no longer omitted, which is a genuine improvement over the textbooks of my parents' generation, but it is sequenced as a problem the country had and then solved, a chapter rather than a load-bearing wall. Labor history, if it appears, appears as a sequence of strikes that were regrettable, violent, and ultimately absorbed into a prosperous middle class. The FBI appears as an agency that catches criminals. The CIA appears, if it appears, as an agency that operates overseas against official enemies. COINTELPRO, if it appears at all, is a paragraph.

Now ask what would have to be true for that sequence to be an adequate description of the country the student actually inhabits. The student would need to leave the course able to explain how a police department's budget is set, how a school-to-prison pipeline is not a metaphor but a set of referral practices and contract incentives, how a fusion center is staffed and what it does with a license-plate file, how an ICE detainer works, and how a private data broker can sell a person's address history to a government that would have needed a warrant to collect the same information directly. The student cannot. The course was not built to produce that competence. It was built to produce a graduate who can identify the three branches of government, name a few amendments, and feel, with some sincerity, that the system is in principle accountable to people like themselves.

That feeling is the manufactured product. I want the word *manufactured* to keep its industrial sense from Chapter 2. Consent in this model is not a spontaneous sentiment that arises from information. It is an output, produced by a process with inputs, waste products, and quality control. The input is a child. The quality control is the test. The waste product is the question the course did not have time for. And the output, if the process runs as designed, is an adult who will read a story about a new surveillance program and experience it as a story about safety, because safety is the frame the classroom installed and the newsroom, under its own filters, will not spend its scarce controversy budget to pry loose.

## Civics as Illusion

What makes this chapter's title deliberate ("the illusion of education") is that the illusion is not that people learn nothing. They learn quite a lot. What they are not generally taught to do is turn the same analytical tools they use on a chemistry problem set onto the institution assigning the homework, or onto the agency three buildings down collecting their browsing history while they do it.

Civics, as it is commonly taught, is a map of official geography. Here is the legislative branch. Here is the executive. Here is the judicial. Here is how a bill becomes a law, a phrase so thoroughly colonized by a Saturday-morning cartoon that many adults can hum the melody and still not describe how a committee chair kills a bill without a vote, how a lobbyist drafts the language that survives, or how a regulatory agency staffed by former industry attorneys writes the rule that determines what the statute will mean in practice. The map is not false in the way a lie is false. It is false in the way a subway map is false if you use it to understand who owns the land the trains run under. It is a diagram of authorized routes. It is not a diagram of power.

The illusion has several layers, and I want to name them so that a reader who has been through this curriculum can locate the moment at which the map replaced the territory.

The first layer is personification. The student is taught that "the government" does things, "the police" protect, "the courts" decide, as if these were characters with consistent motives rather than contested sites staffed by people under incentives. Personification is a teaching convenience. It is also a political convenience, because a character can be good or bad, reformed or corrupt, while a structure can only be described, and description of structure is what the filters are there to postpone.

The second layer is exception. Every institution that has abused its power is introduced, if it is introduced, as an institution that once had a bad season. Japanese internment was a failure. COINTELPRO was a failure. The Church Committee was the correction. The Patriot Act was a response to an emergency and will be reined in. Each episode is narrated as a deviation from a sound baseline, which means the student is trained to experience the next episode as another deviation rather than as the baseline asserting itself. A system that produces the same class of abuse in every generation is not a system with a deviation problem. It is a system with a design.

The third layer is the sacredness of procedure. Due process, warrants, probable cause, the exclusionary rule: these are real legal tools, and I am not arguing that they are meaningless. I am arguing that a civics course which treats them as the description of what agencies actually do, rather than as the description of what agencies can be forced to do when someone with resources sues, is a course in theology. Procedure is the church. Practice is the street. The student is baptized in the church and sent into the street with the impression that the two are the same building.

The fourth layer, and the one that most directly feeds the surveillance half of this chapter, is the moral sorting of the watched and the watchers. The student is taught, sometimes explicitly and more often by tone and example, that surveillance is what happens to criminals, that police attention is a consequence of behavior, and that a person who has done nothing wrong has nothing to fear. This is the most efficient lie in the set, because it converts a structural question into a character question. If you are afraid of being watched, the implication runs, you must have a reason. The person who internalizes that implication will not only fail to resist the panopticon. They will help staff it. They will report the neighbor. They will vote for the camera. They will tell the friend who is worried to relax, because only guilty people worry.

> The education system's role is not simply that it fails to teach students to question these institutions. It actively teaches a version of civics in which the FBI, the police, and the intelligence community are presented as neutral guardians of order.

A student who graduates having read about COINTELPRO as a two-paragraph footnote, if they encounter it at all, is a student equipped to be surprised every time a new version of the same program is exposed, rather than equipped to recognize the pattern on sight. Surprise is a political resource. A surprised public is a public that spends its outrage on the novelty of the abuse rather than on the structure that produced it, and novelty exhausts itself, and the structure remains.

## A Worked Example: What a Textbook Leaves Out

Let me make this concrete, because abstractions about curriculum are how curriculum survives complaint. Imagine a standard high-school unit on the Constitution, three weeks, the amendments, a quiz on which freedom lives in which clause. The First Amendment will be taught as speech, press, religion, assembly, petition. The Fourth will be taught as the right against unreasonable searches and seizures. The Fifth will be taught as the right to remain silent and the protection against being tried twice for the same crime. A diligent student will memorize these. A fortunate student will be asked to apply them to a hypothetical: a locker search, a newspaper censored by a principal, a protest on the courthouse steps. The hypotheticals will be local, physical, and analog. They will not include a geofence warrant that asks a technology company for the identity of every device that was in a park during a demonstration. They will not include a third-party data broker selling a list of people who attended a place of worship, or a clinic, or a union meeting. They will not include the legal doctrine, expanded over the last half-century, under which information you share with a company in order to use a service is treated as information you have voluntarily surrendered, and therefore as information the government may often obtain from the company without obtaining it from you.

The student who aces that quiz knows the text of the Fourth Amendment and does not know the hole that has been opened in it by the combination of commercial data collection and government purchase. That is not a small omission. It is the omission of the primary way the amendment is currently being routed around. Teaching the amendment without teaching the hole is not a neutral simplification. It is an education in a constitutional order that no longer describes the student's life.

Fluency in the text without fluency in the practice is not civic literacy. It is civic recitation. Recitation is what empires prefer, because recitation can be graded, televised, and cited in a speech on the nation's birthday, and it does not produce a population that asks to see the contract with the data broker.

## The Panopticon, Then and Now

Then there is the watching itself. Jeremy Bentham designed, in the late eighteenth century, a prison whose architecture would do the work that a crowd of guards would otherwise have to do. A central tower, a ring of cells, each cell backlit so that the inmate was always visible and the observer in the tower was not. The inmate would not know, at any given moment, whether the observer was looking. The inmate would therefore have to behave as if the observer were always looking. Bentham was proud of the efficiency. He thought he had solved a problem in the economics of punishment. He had also, without intending the career the idea would have, written the operating system of the next two centuries of institutional design.

The panopticon is usually introduced as a curiosity of prison architecture, a drawing in a book, a thought experiment. I want to treat it as a specification. The specification has four requirements. First, the subject must be visible, or believe themselves to be visible. Second, the observer must be unverifiable: the subject cannot confirm, in the moment, whether observation is actually occurring. Third, the architecture must be cheaper than the older method of watching, which is to say it must reduce the number of watchers required per watched body. Fourth, the subject must internalize the possibility of observation thoroughly enough that the architecture can occasionally go empty without the compliance collapsing. Those four requirements can be met with stone and light, as Bentham proposed. They can also be met with fiber optic cable, a terms-of-service agreement, a fusion-center terminal, and a public that has been taught, in civics class, that only the guilty have reason to mind.

Edward Snowden's *Permanent Record* is not a policy paper. It is a confession, which is what makes it useful here. He describes walking into the NSA's contractor world and discovering that the collection of ordinary Americans' communications was not the exception, it was the default setting, hoovered up under the theory that storing everything and asking permission later was more efficient than the reverse. That is Bentham's panopticon rebuilt in fiber optic cable: the prisoner does not need to know exactly when the guard is watching, only that the guard could be watching at any moment, for the desired behavior to become self-enforcing. Add ICE's expanding data-sharing agreements with local police, license-plate readers, and facial recognition contracts, and the tower in the middle of the yard is no longer a metaphor. It is an org chart.

Bentham's inmate at least knew he was in a prison. The modern subject of collection is told, daily, that they are in a free country, and the telling is not entirely false. They may speak. They may vote, within the narrowed range Chapter 4 will describe. They may, if they have the resources, hire a lawyer and contest a specific search. What they may not do, except at the cost of a Snowden-shaped life, is inspect the tower. The unverifiability Bentham designed into the architecture has been upgraded. It is no longer merely that you cannot tell whether the guard is looking. It is that you cannot tell, with any confidence, what is being collected, where it is stored, which agencies it is shared with, which contractors have a copy, or which future administration will inherit the archive. The inmate has been given a flag and a civics textbook and sent to live in the cell as if it were a house.

I will not reconstruct, from the outside, the interior of any collection program, and I will not offer advice on how to hide from one. That is not this book's project, and it is not a project I am willing to lend these pages to. What I am willing to do is take the shape he described seriously, because the shape matches the specification Bentham wrote and the theory Foucault later generalized. Matching is not proof of every detail. It is a reason not to file the confession under thriller and return to the textbook.

## Foucault's Equation: p versus p-hat

Bentham designed the panopticon's architecture, but it was Michel Foucault, in *Discipline and Punish*, who turned that architecture into a general theory of how modern power actually operates. Foucault's argument was that the prison, the school, the hospital, and the factory all converged on the same underlying mechanism sometime around the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: a shift away from the old sovereign model of punishment, a public spectacle meant to terrify a crowd into obedience, toward a quieter and far more efficient model built on constant, internalized visibility. His phrase for the result was that visibility itself becomes a trap. Once a subject cannot be certain whether they are being observed at any given moment, they begin supervising themselves, and the institution no longer needs to expend the effort of watching everyone, because everyone is now doing the watching's job for free.

That mechanism can be stated with the same precision Chapter 2 applied to Kaczynski. Let *p* denote the actual probability that a given subject is being observed at any moment, and let *p̂* denote that subject's perceived probability of being observed. Foucault's insight was that compliance is a function of *p̂*, not of *p*, and that a well-designed disciplinary system can drive *p* toward a small fraction of the population while still holding *p̂* close to one for everybody. The cost of the watching apparatus scales roughly with the number of guards, cameras, or analysts a system employs, call that cost function *W(N)*, but the compliance it purchases does not scale with *W(N)* at all once *p̂* has been successfully installed. It scales instead with how thoroughly the subject has internalized the possibility of being watched, which is a psychological quantity the state gets for nearly nothing after the initial architecture is built.

Write it once, so it cannot be waved away as atmosphere:

> Compliance = f(*p̂*), and a cheap system is one that keeps *p̂* ≈ 1 while driving *p*, and therefore *W(N)*, toward a minimum.

That is the efficiency Foucault was describing, and it is also, not coincidentally, the exact efficiency a modern data-collection program achieves. ICE, the NSA, and a private data broker do not need to watch every citizen every second. They need only maintain a *p̂* close enough to one that the population polices its own browsing history, its own protest attendance, its own speech, on the state's behalf and at the state's cost of essentially zero.

A worked example, because an equation that stays on the page is an ornament. Suppose a city installs a modest number of automatic license-plate readers on major roads, enough to cover a fraction of actual trips. The actual probability *p* that any given drive is captured may be well below one. The perceived probability *p̂* among drivers who have heard that the cameras exist, who have seen a story about a car being identified after a crime, who have been told by a friend that "they have those cameras everywhere now," can sit close to one. The city does not need to read every plate. It needs the rumor of the plates, the occasional confirmed identification, and a public already trained to treat traffic enforcement as a natural feature of the landscape. The compliance (slower driving in camera corridors, second thoughts about which parking lot to use, a slight hesitation before attending a rally whose location is served by a monitored road) is purchased at the price of the rumor plus a handful of devices. Foucault would have recognized the bargain immediately. Bentham would have asked why it took us so long.

I keep the *p* versus *p̂* distinction in front of the reader because it is the distinction that official language works hardest to erase. Official language talks about what is collected, under what authority, with what oversight, as if the relevant quantity were *p*, the actual watching. The political quantity is *p̂*. A program can be legally narrow, actually applied to a small number of people, and still produce a large *p̂* if the public believes the program is broader than it is, or if the public cannot find out how broad it is and therefore has to assume the worst in order to stay safe. Unverifiability, Bentham's second requirement, is not a flaw in this design. It is the design. A transparent surveillance system would have to justify itself continuously. An unverifiable one justifies itself once, as a necessity, and then lives forever in the imagination of the people who cannot inspect it.

The school is one of the sites where *p̂* is installed early, before a person has the language to notice the installation. Hall passes, cameras in corridors, software that flags search terms on a school-issued laptop, a resource officer in the lobby: each of these may be defended, in isolation, as a response to a real harm. I am not going to pretend that schools have no safety problems, or that a parent who wants a locked door is a dupe. I am going to notice that a generation trained to experience constant visibility as the background condition of learning is a generation that will not find constant visibility strange when it follows them out of the building. The school is where *p̂* is normalized. The rest of the disciplinary apparatus harvests that normalization.

## Permanent Record

Snowden titled his book *Permanent Record* with a precision that the rest of the culture has mostly declined to sit with. A record, in the old analog sense, was a thing that could be lost, flooded, misfiled, or destroyed in a fire. Its permanence was an aspiration of clerks. A digital record, copied across servers, backed up, mirrored, sold, and inherited by the next contractor when the last one is acquired, is permanent in a way no eighteenth-century archive could be. The file on you does not die when the officer who opened it retires. It does not die when the program that collected it is "ended," a word that in this domain often means rebranded, migrated, or absorbed into a successor with a less radioactive name. It does not die when you die. It becomes, at that point, a historical document about a person who can no longer contest it.

He describes a culture in which the default was ingest first and filter later, because storage had become cheap and the legal and political costs of being accused of missing something had become expensive. That inversion (cheaper to keep everything than to decide what to keep) is the industrial revolution of surveillance. Chapter 2 described an industrial system that converted human time into shareholder return with increasing efficiency. The collection apparatus Snowden described converts human communication into stored potential, a reservoir that can be queried when a justification appears. The justification can be a crime. It can be a visa application. It can be a tip. It can be a model that says a pattern of behavior is anomalous. The reservoir does not need to know, at the time of collection, which of these it will be asked to serve. That is the point of a reservoir.

The sanctioned story, the one the civics textbook and the official spokesperson tell, is that collection is targeted, overseen, and aimed at people who have given the state a reason. The operational reality Snowden described was a system whose architecture made targeting a later stage rather than a first principle. You cannot, from outside the rooms, adjudicate every claim in his account. You can notice that the architecture he describes is the architecture that would exist if Bentham and Foucault were given modern storage, modern legal doctrines about third-party data, and a political culture that had already installed *p̂* in the population through schools, airports, and the evening news. The account fits the theory. Dismissing it because it is uncomfortable is not analysis. It is the filter working.

The sanctioned story is not always a lie in the sense of a sentence that is false. It is more often a story that is true of a narrow case and then offered as a description of the system. A spokesperson says that a program is legal, overseen, and targeted. *Legal* means a statute or interpretation has been made to cover the practice, which is not the same as the practice having been described to the public when the statute was passed. *Overseen* means someone with a clearance has been told something, which is not the same as the public being able to evaluate it. *Targeted* means a justification exists at the point of a query, which is not the same as collection itself being limited to people for whom a justification exists. A reservoir can be queried in a targeted way. That does not make the reservoir a targeting system. It makes it a reservoir with a search bar.

Snowden's own trajectory is instructive on the education half of this chapter. He describes himself, before his years inside the intelligence community, as a patriot in the conventional sense, someone who trusted that the agencies collecting data were doing so within the bounds the public had been told applied. What radicalized him was not a seminar in anarcho-syndicalism. It was the distance between the briefing and the terminal. I keep returning to that distance because it is the same distance a civics student is asked not to notice: the distance between the amendment on the quiz and the program in the basement, between the guardian in the textbook and the contractor at the rack of servers. A country that produces Snowdens only as accidents of proximity is a country whose education system is doing the job this chapter says it is doing. It is producing believers, and then hoping the believers never get a look at the machine room.

> What radicalized him was not ideology, it was proximity: watching the scale of the collection from the inside, and recognizing the distance between what the public had been told and what was actually being built.

That gap, between the sanctioned story and the operational reality, is this chapter's real subject. The panopticon does not need every citizen watched every moment to function. It needs only enough citizens uncertain whether they are being watched, and enough of the rest confident that surveillance only happens to people who deserve it, for the tower in the yard to do its work without a single extra guard being hired.

## ICE, Data Brokers, and the Warrant-Shaped Hole

Immigration and Customs Enforcement is not the only agency in this chapter, and it is not the origin of the American surveillance impulse. It is, at present, one of the clearest demonstrations of how the impulse has been privatized, localized, and routed around the warrant requirement that the civics textbook still treats as a load-bearing wall. I am going to describe this in structural terms, with the kinds of facts that have been reported in public for years, because the point is not to surprise the reader with a secret. The point is to name a pattern that has been sitting in the open, waiting for a curriculum that would treat it as civic knowledge rather than as someone else's controversy.

ICE, like other enforcement agencies, does not have to build every observational tool in-house. It can buy. The commercial data-broker industry (firms that assemble, from public records, consumer transactions, utility files, location streams, and the residue of ordinary digital life, a queryable picture of a person) exists to sell that picture to whoever will pay, including governments. The legal convenience is almost elegant. If the government collected the same mosaic directly from the person, it might need a warrant, or at least a subpoena the person would know about. If the government buys the mosaic from a company that collected it under a terms-of-service agreement the person clicked in order to use email, or a car, or a phone, the collection is recoded as a market transaction. The warrant-shaped hole is the space where the Fourth Amendment's text still exists and the Fourth Amendment's practice has been rerouted through a checkout page.

I want to stay with the word *broker*, because it is more honest than *tech*. A broker does not have to invent a product. A broker takes something that is already moving (here, information about where people live, who they know, what they buy, which towers their devices talk to) and makes it available to a buyer who would rather not collect it under their own letterhead. The buyer gets the file without having to be the face of the collection. The broker gets a government contract. The person the file describes gets, if they are lucky, a privacy policy written in a language no civics course taught them to read.

Local police enter this picture through sharing agreements, joint task forces, and the quiet interoperability of databases. A county that wants to cooperate does not, in the contemporary arrangement, need to reinvent a surveillance apparatus. It needs to plug in. License-plate readers purchased for "stolen vehicle recovery" generate a travel history. That history can be of interest to more than one agency. Facial-recognition contracts sold as a way to identify the unconscious patient in an emergency room can be queried for other purposes once the faces are in the system. I am not claiming that every department uses every tool in the most aggressive way available. I am claiming that the architecture does not require every department to do so. It requires only that the data exist, that the legal pathway to query it exist, and that *p̂* among the populations most likely to be queried sit high enough that those populations police themselves.

The populations most likely to be queried are not a random sample, any more than the omissions from the civics textbook were a random sample. Immigrant communities, mixed-status families, people who work in the cash economy, people who live in over-policed neighborhoods, people who have been arrested whether or not they were convicted: these are the testing grounds, the places where a tool is normalized before it is generalized. This is an old pattern. It is the pattern of COINTELPRO, which I will come to in a moment, and it is the pattern of every disciplinary technology Foucault described, which is tested on the prisoner, the patient, the student, the soldier, before it is offered to the public as a convenience. If you want to know what a surveillance tool will do in ten years, look at what it is already doing to the people with the least capacity to refuse it now.

I will say again, because this book is a critique and not a manual, that none of this is an instruction in how to defeat an agency, evade an arrest, or conceal a crime. It is a description of a political economy of information, in which a public good (the constitutional restraint on searches) has been partially enclosed by a private market and then rented back to the state. The enclosure is the scandal. The rental is the business model. The civics illusion is what keeps the scandal from being taught as the business model it is.

## Police, Partnerships, and the Org Chart in the Yard

Police, in the official geography of civics, are local. They are the officer at the intersection, the department whose chief is hired by a mayor, the force that answers, in theory, to a city. That locality is real in some respects: a town of four thousand and a city of four million do not run the same department. It is less real in the respects that matter to this chapter. Training curricula travel. Equipment vendors travel. Federal grants travel, and they travel with conditions. Information-sharing platforms travel. A reform that is won in one city can be routed around by a task force that sits in a different jurisdiction and draws on a federal database. The badge is local. The stack is not.

Fusion centers, built out after 2001 as nodes for sharing terrorism-related intelligence among agencies, became, as such nodes tend to become, general-purpose. A center that can receive a suspicious-activity report, a license-plate file, and a set of commercially purchased records is a center that can serve many missions. Mission creep is not a scandal in the cinematic sense. It is the ordinary life of a capability. Once the capability exists, every subsequent problem looks like a use case. The civics student is taught that agencies have mandates. The operational reality is that agencies have capabilities, and mandates are the language in which capabilities are explained after the fact.

The partnership model is the solvent that dissolves the map of three levels of government. A local department participates in a federal task force. A federal agency embeds in a local department. A private vendor hosts the database that both of them query. The person who is queried does not experience three levels. They experience a stop, a knock, a detention, a request for identification that seems to know more than it should. The org chart in the yard (the tower at the center of Bentham's ring) is this partnership structure. You cannot, from the cell, tell which agency is in the tower on a given day. You cannot tell whether the observer is a sworn officer, a contractor, or an algorithm scoring a file. Unverifiability, again, is not a failure of the design. It is the design.

> Add ICE's expanding data-sharing agreements with local police, license-plate readers, and facial recognition contracts, and the tower in the middle of the yard is no longer a metaphor. It is an org chart.

Fusion centers, built out after 2001 as terrorism-intelligence nodes, became general-purpose, as such nodes tend to. Once the capability exists, every subsequent problem looks like a use case. The civics student is taught that agencies have mandates. The operational reality is that agencies have capabilities. Police have always kept files. What has changed is the cost. When a file is a query against a broker's store, a cheap file is a file that will be opened. That is the same economics Bentham was proud of.

## Malcolm X and COINTELPRO

Malcolm X's experience is worth adding here precisely because it predates the digital infrastructure Snowden exposed by half a century, which is what makes it useful: it shows the surveillance impulse is not a technology problem. Technology only made it more efficient. The FBI's COINTELPRO program treated Malcolm X, and organizers like him, as threats to be monitored, infiltrated, and disrupted well before a fiber-optic cable or a data center existed to do the watching automatically. Agents attended his meetings, recorded his associations, and built files on a man whose primary offense was organizing people who had been failed by the "big club" described later in this book. The tools have since been automated. The ideology behind their use has not: a state that treats organized dissent as a security problem will always find a justification for watching the people doing the organizing, and it will always describe that watching as protective rather than political.

I want the historical texture here, not as ornament, but as an antidote to the idea that we are dealing with a post-2001 novelty. COINTELPRO, as a named program, ran from the 1950s into the early 1970s, and it targeted Communist Party members, civil rights organizations, Black nationalist groups, the American Indian Movement, and a range of New Left formations, among others. It used infiltration, anonymous letters, the spreading of rumors designed to break marriages and friendships, the feeding of stories to friendly journalists, and, in documented cases, the kind of disruption that does not stay on paper. The Church Committee, in the mid-1970s, put a portion of this on the public record. The civics textbook, if it mentions the Church Committee at all, tends to mention it as proof that the system corrects itself. A reader who stops there can go on believing in the exception layer I described above. A reader who continues notices that the correction was partial, that the files were not abolished as a practice, that the legal authorities for domestic intelligence were rewritten rather than relinquished, and that the organizational reflex (treat political organization among the dispossessed as a security problem) did not retire when J. Edgar Hoover did.

Malcolm X is a useful figure for this book for reasons that go beyond the file the FBI kept on him, and I will return to him in Chapter 4 when the subject is the ballot. Here, the relevant fact is that a man who was teaching people to take their own condition seriously, to stop waiting for a savior, and to organize, was treated as a counterintelligence target. The content of his speeches was political. The state's response was framed as security. That recoding (politics into security) is the master stroke of the disciplinary apparatus, because once a movement is a security problem, the watching of it is no longer a First Amendment event. It is a protective event. The civics student is taught to value both the First Amendment and protection. The recoding forces a choice that the student has not been trained to see as a choice. Protection wins. It almost always does, when the person being protected-from is someone the student has been taught to think of as a disturbance.

Fred Hampton, murdered in his bed in 1969 by a raid that relied on an informant and a floor plan, is the end of a spectrum that begins with a file. I mention him not to collapse every surveillance practice into assassination, which would be dishonest, but to refuse the opposite collapse, in which surveillance is treated as information management with no relationship to what is done with the information. Files are not inert. They are the condition of later decisions: who is raided, who is discredited, who is offered a deal, who is left alone. A panopticon that only watches is a panopticon in a thought experiment. A panopticon in history feeds the institutions that knock on doors.

> The infrastructure changes. The instinct underneath it, the state's discomfort with populations it cannot fully predict, does not.

That continuity matters because it undercuts the comforting idea that mass surveillance is a recent overreach, a post-9/11 anomaly that a sufficiently outraged public could vote away. The infrastructure changes. The instinct underneath it does not. ICE's current data-sharing arrangements with local police departments and its use of commercial data brokers to route around warrant requirements are simply COINTELPRO with better bandwidth, applied now to immigrant communities the way it was once applied to civil rights organizers, and to whichever community draws the state's attention next.

I say "simply" and I want to qualify it immediately. The analogy is structural, not moral in every particular. COINTELPRO was a named program of disruption directed at political organizations. Contemporary data brokerage and immigration enforcement are a market and a statutory apparatus directed at a legal status and, in practice, at a racialized population. The people in the files are not the same. The harm is not identical. What is the same is the conversion of a political problem (who belongs, who organizes, who refuses the terms) into a technical problem of identification, prediction, and disruption, and the wrapping of that conversion in the language of safety. If you can see that conversion in 1967, you can see it in 2026. If you cannot see it in 1967, because your textbook gave you two paragraphs and a reassurance, you will have trouble seeing it now, except as a controversy about borders, or crime, or the proper respect due to officers. The controversy is the costume. The conversion is the plot.

## Big Brother Was Always a User Interface

Orwell's usefulness is real and has been, I think, partly exhausted by overuse. The phrase Big Brother now functions as a reflex, a way to signal that one is the sort of person who minds cameras, and reflexes are not analysis. What remains useful in the novel, for this chapter's purposes, is not the boot or the rats. It is the television that watches you back, and the idea that the watching would be woven into the furniture of ordinary life until it stopped feeling like watching and started feeling like the room. We do not have a Ministry of Truth in the theatrical sense. We have a feed, a set of platforms, a set of agencies, a set of contractors, and a civics language that has not been updated since the furniture was analog.

The user interface is the contemporary achievement. Bentham's inmate could see the tower. The user of a modern device sees a map, a weather report, a prompt to review a restaurant, a notification that a package has arrived. The collection is not the thing on the screen. The collection is the condition of the thing on the screen, the bargain that was struck, usually without negotiation, so that the screen would be useful enough to carry everywhere. I am not telling the reader to throw the device in a river. I am noting that a panopticon which presents as a convenience will be invited into more rooms than a panopticon which presents as a tower, and that the invitation will be experienced as a series of small, reasonable decisions. Each decision is reasonable. The accumulation is the architecture.

There is no man named Big Brother to vote out. There is a stack: a device, a firm, a broker, a contract, an agency, a partnership, a local department, a prosecutor's office, a detention facility, a court that may or may not ever see the mosaic that started the chain. The stack does not have a face. The stack has a terms-of-service update. A public trained to look for a face (a tyrant, a corrupt chief, a rogue analyst) will miss the stack, and missing the stack is how the stack survives elections. You can vote a man out. You cannot, under the political system Chapter 4 will describe, easily vote a stack out, because the stack is not on the ballot, and the people who are on the ballot have been, as a rule, funded by the firms that own pieces of the stack.

Orwell imagined a state that forbade unofficial speech and required official speech. Chomsky and Herman described a system that does not need to forbid, because the range of speech that reaches a mass audience has already been narrowed by ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, and a designated enemy. The American achievement, if it can be called that, is to have built a panopticon that does not require a Ministry, and a propaganda system that does not require a censor. The watching is mostly legal. The filtering is mostly commercial. The public is mostly sure that it is free, because it can, in fact, say a great many things, including the things in this book, and the capacity to say them is not nothing. It is also not the same as the capacity to find out who has a copy of the saying, or to make the saying matter in the rooms where the stack is governed.

## The Closed Loop

The two halves reinforce each other. Education produces a public whose *p̂* is already partly installed and whose sense of legitimacy is already attached to the watchers. Surveillance produces a public that has reason to be careful, and careful people are less likely to demand that the curriculum change, because demanding that the curriculum change is itself a visible act. The loop is not a conspiracy meeting. It is a pair of equilibria. Schools that teach the sanctioned story produce adults who do not storm the school board over the sanctioned story. Agencies that are not asked hard questions in the classroom become agencies that are not asked hard questions in the hearing room, except by a minority that can be framed as unserious. The minority is not always wrong to be angry. Anger without a map of the stack is still, too often, recoverable as a news cycle.

Peter Joseph's language of structural violence, which I used in Chapter 2 for the economy of scarcity, applies here with only a change of scenery. A person who does not know they are in a file can still be harmed by the file. A person who knows they might be in a file, and who therefore does not go to the meeting, does not sign the petition, does not visit the relative, has been harmed by *p̂* whether or not *p* was ever pointed at them. That harm does not look like a baton. It looks like an absence: the meeting that is smaller than it would have been, the organization that never forms, the journalist who does not make the call, the student who does not write the essay that would have named the stack. Structural violence in this domain is the violence of things that do not happen. It will not show up in a crime statistic. It will show up, if you know how to look, in a civic life that is quieter than the country's problems would predict.

The anarcho-syndicalist thread of this book, which I named in the introduction as a tradition of organizing production from the bottom rather than owning it from the top, has a specific implication here. A surveillance apparatus that is owned downward (by agencies the public cannot inspect, by firms the public cannot vote on, by partnerships the public did not design) will not become a public utility of safety merely because the word safety is used in the press release. The question this tradition asks of a factory is: who controls the machine, and who eats the harm when the machine fails? The question it asks of a panopticon is the same. Who holds *p*, who manufactures *p̂*, and who is told that the arrangement is the price of living in a free country?

I am not calling for a raid on a data center. I am calling, in the only register this book allows itself, for a change in what is treated as ordinary knowledge. A graduate who cannot explain a data broker, a fusion center, a partnership that turns a local officer into a federal immigration node, a geofence warrant, or COINTELPRO has not been educated in the country they live in. They have been educated in a brochure. The brochure is well written. It has a flag on the cover. It will not help them when the file is already open.

## What the Homework Never Asks

I will end this chapter where the student is asked to end: with the assignment. The assignment, in the system I have been describing, is to reproduce the map. Name the branches. Quote the amendment. Distinguish a right from a privilege. Explain, in a paragraph, why checks and balances prevent tyranny. The assignment is not: take this county's police budget and this county's school budget and explain the ratio. The assignment is not: find the vendor on this department's facial-recognition contract and say who sits on the vendor's board. The assignment is not: read the Church Committee's findings and then read last year's privacy impact assessment for a fusion center and say what, if anything, has structurally changed. The assignment is not: write the Fourth Amendment as it is practiced, not as it is printed.

Those un-assigned tasks are not mysteries. They are the tasks that would make the two beliefs difficult to hold. A student who completed them would still be free, in the ordinary senses. They would also be unable to accept, without a fight in their own head, that they are watched for their own good by institutions they have never been permitted to study with the same seriousness they were required to study the mitochondria.

I do not think a better worksheet, by itself, dissolves a panopticon. I think a public that has never been given the worksheet is a public that can be told, every time a new surface of the stack is exposed, that this too is an anomaly, a rogue, a misunderstanding, a necessary exception, a price of safety, a thing that does not happen to people like you. The education system does not have to love the stack to serve it. It only has to keep the stack off the test.

The next chapter is about the club that funds both the test and, in more than a few cases, the stack, and about the ceremony in which a watched, well-schooled population is invited to choose between two members of the club and to call the choice freedom. If this chapter has done its work, the reader will enter that ceremony unable to mistake it for a self-governing act. If this chapter has not done its work, the reader will vote, feel counted, and remain, as Bentham's inmate remained, visible to a tower they were told was a lighthouse.
