# Arguments for An Abolishment of Corporate-Owned America

## An Anarcho-Syndicalist Manifesto

Alexander Caro

2026

The two-hundred-and-fiftieth year of the United States of America

---

## Author's Note

The following is not a violent nor call to action piece; rather a political critique. Clarification of “abolishment” being a dissolution of American corporations and a critique of the pyramid scheme and rat race disguised as free market capitalism. A peaceful means to put power back into the hands of We The People.

I want that paragraph to do more than sit in the front of the book as a lawyer's belt. It is the constraint under which every later chapter is written, including the chapter that takes as its subject a regional flashpoint and the precipice of a wider war. Abolishment, throughout, means the dissolution of corporate rule over daily life, not the destruction of persons, not a night of settling scores, and not a program of sabotage. Dissolution is an institutional word. It names the ending of a particular ownership relation (the downward ownership of production by shareholders, and of policy by the state-corporate hybrid that protects those shareholders) and the construction of a different relation in its place. The different relation is named in the introduction and argued in the conclusion: workers' councils, federated production, the people who do the work setting the terms of the work.

Peaceful is not a marketing adjective. It is a refusal to treat other human beings as fuel for a cause, including this one. Albert Camus occupies later pages for that reason. A critique that ends by blessing a sacred violence has not abolished the machine. It has applied for a job there.

I will be as plain as the later chapters allow. Not violent means that nothing in these pages is an instruction to harm a person, a crowd, or a building. Not a call to action means that the book does not appoint the reader as an operative and does not pretend that a sentence can substitute for the slow work of building institutions. Abolishment means the dissolution of corporate rule: the ending of the downward ownership relation that currently sets the pace of work, the content of news, the terms of a lease, the availability of medicine, and, as Chapter 5 will argue, the slope of a war. Dissolution is not a synonym for wreckage. It is the replacement of one set of decision-rights with another. Peaceful means the replacement is accomplished by organization, by withdrawal of legitimacy, by the construction of councils and federations, and not by a romance of the street. We The People means the people who already do the country's work, not a mascot and not a mob.

We The People, in this note and in the conclusion, is not a costume and not a campaign. It is a claim about who is entitled to constitute the decisions that currently sit inside what Chapter 4 calls the big club. The people who feed, nurse, wire, teach, drive, clean, and bury the country are already the country. The book is an argument that they are entitled to the decisions as well as the labor.


I have dated the work to 2026 because the date is the argument's occasion, not its decoration. A historian of empires, Sir John Glubb, observed that the large historical cases he surveyed tended to last on the order of two hundred and fifty years. I do not treat the number as physics. I treat it as a rhyme the present is living inside, and I have written accordingly.

> The Earth is littered with the ruins of empires that once believed they were eternal.
>
> Camille Paglia

Paglia's sentence is the epigraph because it is the one fact the later chapters keep rediscovering in different rooms: the factory, the classroom, the file, the ballot box, and the overseas slope. Eternity is a brand. Ruins are an output. The book is a political critique of the output, offered while the brand is still being printed on the two-hundred-and-fiftieth anniversary merchandise.

## Preface

We'll begin with the start of the nation, beginning with the all too familiar story of the colonists fleeing Great Britain and establishing independence on July 4th, 1776. It is apparent that a small detail of the colonists being slave owners has been brushed under the rug for as long as the story has been told; slave owners who wanted to be free, and preached all men are created equal. The beginning set the tone for the absolute circus show that would follow over the next 25 decades.

The story is familiar because it is taught as origin rather than as structure. Origin stories ask a reader to feel gratitude, or grievance, or both, and then to stop. Structure asks a reader to notice that the thing being originated had a shape, and that the shape can recur without the original costumes. The shape, in 1776, was a settler population that wanted relief from a distant extraction and was prepared to announce universal principles while retaining a labor system that made those principles a private club. I do not rehearse this in order to win a contest of denunciation with the dead. I rehearse it because the later chapters will be accused, by people who have not read them, of being ungrateful toward a founding that was already, in its bones, a dual ledger: a public transcript of equality and an operational transcript of ownership.

The taxes that occupy the first pages of Chapter 1 (the Sugar Act, the Stamp Act, the Townshend Acts, the Tea Act) are usually taught as a list to be memorized and then emotionally associated with a harbor and a clock tower. Taught as structure, they are something colder. A distant authority extracted value from a population that had no seat at the table where the extraction was decided. The Tea Act, in particular, was a bailout dressed as a tax cut, designed to prop up the East India Company by giving a failing corporation a privileged position in a market it also supplied. The people who dumped the tea were not rejecting a bargain price. They were rejecting a system in which a favored company wrote the rules of the game it played. Anyone who has watched a twenty-first-century bailout, a twenty-first-century regulatory capture, or a twenty-first-century platform that is both marketplace and competitor has seen the family resemblance. The resemblance is the book's subject. The harbor is only the first photograph of it.

Twenty-five decades is a long circus. It includes the expansion that Glubb would have filed under conquest, the industrialization that Chapter 2 files under the re-engineering of the human day, the wars that compiled a Grand Area, the financializations that taught a public to socialize risk and privatize profit, the surveillance architectures that taught a public to watch itself, and the duopoly that taught a public to experience a screened choice as sovereignty. It also includes real goods: labor victories, civil-rights victories, expansions of the franchise, expansions of literacy, expansions of the claim (still unpaid) that the preamble's "people" might someday mean the people. A critique that cannot admit the goods will be filed as a tantrum. A celebration that cannot admit the dual ledger will be filed as a commercial. This book is the former risk. I would rather be accused of a tantrum than sell the commercial in the anniversary year.

The preface is the place to say what the book is not, before the introduction says what it is. It is not a memoir, though it is written in the first person because a political critique that pretends to have no location is usually located in the club. It is not a textbook, though it borrows from history, political science, sociology, philosophy, and the documentary record of the intelligence services. It is not a policy white paper for a campaign, because campaigns are one of the filters later chapters describe. It is not a brief for a foreign government, a domestic party, or a military service, and Chapter 5 exists in part to make that refusal expensive to misread. It is not a manual. There are no operational instructions in these pages, no targeting suggestions, no recipes for harm, no contact lists, no invitations to a street. There is a tradition, named in the introduction, and there is a measure, borrowed from Camus, and there is a date.

The date is June 2026, one month before the two-hundred-and-fiftieth year. If the rhyme Glubb noticed has any use, it is not as a countdown clock. Countdown clocks belong to the feed. The use is as a permission to look at the present as a late stage rather than as an exception. Late stages are vain. They prefer to be told they are exceptions. This preface declines.

A late stage also prefers to be told that its foreign crises are unprecedented. They are not. They are the overseas face of the same dual ledger the harbor photographed. Chapter 5 will name Iran and Israel as a flashpoint in 2026 and will refuse, at length, to become a brief for either government. I flag that refusal here so that a reader who came for a jersey can leave with the jersey still in the bag. The book is a critique of corporate-owned America. It will not be drafted, in the preface or later, into a war. It will not be drafted into a quietism that treats the people under that war as an abstraction either. Camus's measure, which the introduction will name, is the refusal of both drafts.

## Introduction


I'd like to begin by stating that it is currently June 2026, one month before the 250th year of the United States of America. This will uncoincidentally be the topic of the next chapter. If an individual had by some means been in a coma and woken up suddenly, I do not think it far-fetched for said individual to voluntarily go back into the coma. Throughout this work I will utilize numerous different sources, from topics of sociology, political science, technology, biology, philosophy, linguistics and a light drizzle of fantasy. I would like to briefly mention that I draw inspiration from the works, and will quote, from the likes of Noam Chomsky, Peter Joseph, Albert Camus, Edward Snowden, Malcolm X and lastly Theodore Kaczynski.

The coma sentence is not a joke about the news cycle, or not only that. It is a claim about what a late-stage empire feels like from the inside when the interior arrangements of daily life (the wage, the lease, the hospital bill, the feed, the camera, the ballot) have come uncoupled from the speeches that describe them. A person who woke into 2026 from, say, the middle of the Age of Affluence would recognize the brand names of the speeches and would not recognize the distribution of their promises. That gap, between brand and distribution, is the gap Chomsky spent a career measuring in documents, the gap Snowden walked into as an employee, the gap Malcolm X named as theater, the gap Camus named as the moment a cause stops being answerable to a face.

I should also name the tradition this book argues from, since the chapters ahead borrow a specific vocabulary of workers' councils, direct action, and organization without a state or a boss rather than any single thinker's brand of socialism. That tradition is anarcho-syndicalism: the belief that production should be organized by the people doing the producing, federated upward through voluntary unions rather than owned downward through shareholders, and that the state apparatus propping up the modern corporation is no more legitimate, and no more permanent, than the corporation itself.

I have kept Marx off the list of influences deliberately. The description of a class that owns nothing but its capacity to work, positioned against a class that owns the means of turning that work into profit, is available without accepting a party that claimed history as a license. Camus wrote *The Rebel* against that license. This book takes the description and refuses the license. The alternative is not a new sovereign and not a five-year plan administered by people who do not do the work. It is the federation of the people who do.

Kaczynski is on the list for a reason I want stated in the introduction, so that a reader who would otherwise close the book at the name can decide whether to continue on honest terms. His manifesto argued that industrial-technological systems purchase convenience with autonomy, and that the resulting complexity cannot be reformed piecemeal because the cost of reversal grows with the complexity. I think the diagnosis is serious. I reject the treatment he chose, without remainder, without romance, and without the cheap online habit of treating a rejection as a formality. A correct description of a trend line does not authorize a method. Chapter 2 will say this with more machinery. The introduction's job is to put the refusal on the first pages, where it cannot be said to have been buried.

Snowden is on the list as a witness rather than as a mascot. *Permanent Record* is a confession about a default setting: collection first, permission later, the public issued a story that did not match the operational ledger. Chapter 3 treats the domestic face of that ledger. Chapter 5 treats the overseas face, including the use of signals intelligence to inform targeting decisions the public cannot evaluate. In both rooms the point is the same. A people asked to consent to an arrangement they are not permitted to see are not a people in the sense the preamble requires. They are a population.

Chomsky is on the list twice over, which the chapters will make obvious. With Edward Herman, in *Manufacturing Consent*, he described a propaganda model in which the news does not need an overt censor to sort a public into compliance. Ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak, and the dominant ideological fear of the moment are sufficient. The model's most portable finding, for this book, is the distinction between worthy and unworthy victims: suffering that is personalized and attributed to an official enemy, and comparable suffering that is abstracted because attributing it honestly would implicate an ally or the reporting nation. Chapter 3 uses the model on the classroom and the news. Chapter 5 uses it on a flashpoint. In *Hegemony or Survival*, Chomsky described American foreign policy since 1945 as organized around primacy (the insistence on being the deciding voice in every strategically relevant region) even when primacy and the survival of the species come into tension. The title is a fork. A decadent empire is poorly equipped to notice it is choosing.

Peter Joseph is on the list for structural violence: not the baton, the scarcity manufactured in the midst of abundance because scarcity is profitable. The factory did not only industrialize labor. It industrialized need. Advertising, planned obsolescence, and the wage relation manufacture a hunger the system is positioned to sell the cure for. Chapter 2 carries that claim through the gig-economy costume of the old relation. Chapter 4 carries it through a legislature whose outputs track donors. Chapter 5 carries it through sanctions and reconstruction, the slow violence and the invoice.

Malcolm X is on the list because he understood leverage. "The Ballot or the Bullet" is taught, when it is taught, as a mood. It is a structural argument: voting without leverage is theater, and a bloc that can be assumed will be taken for granted by the party assuming it and ignored by the party that has written it off. Chapter 4 generalizes the structure without stealing the particular history it was built from. Chapter 3 notes that the FBI's files on him predate the fiber-optic panopticon, which is how a reader knows the panopticon is not a gadget problem.

Camus is on the list as the book's conscience, which is a pompous way of saying that a political critique without a limit will become an office of the thing it describes. *The Rebel* distinguishes a refusal that affirms a human measure from a revolution that makes history sacred and the present population fuel. Chapter 2 borrows his Sisyphus for the workday. Chapter 5 borrows *The Rebel* for the slope of escalation. The conclusion borrows the yes that accompanies the no. I am not using him as a mascot for a bloc of states. I am using him as a writer who understood what happens to a face when a cause becomes absolute.

Glubb is the framework rather than a member of the inspiration list, and he occupies Chapter 1 at length because the rest of the book is, in effect, a tour of his sixth stage. The Age of Pioneers, the Age of Conquests, the Age of Commerce, the Age of Affluence, the Age of Intellect, and the Age of Decadence: six rooms, one building. Most Americans alive in 2026 have only ever lived in affluence's aftermath, which they have been taught to call normality. Intellect, in Glubb's warning, produces brilliant critique and very little unified action. Decadence is what happens when the critique has been filed and the outward posture of conquest continues after the interior has begun to hollow. This book is an attempt to spend the intellect on something other than the filing.

The chapters that follow are sequential on purpose. Chapter 1 gives the 250-year frame and the name late state capitalism. Chapter 2 gives the industrial engine and the proletarian condition. Chapter 3 gives the classroom and the camera. Chapter 4 gives the club and the decorative ballot. Chapter 5 gives the overseas slope, with Iran and Israel named as a flashpoint and not as a pair of teams the reader is required to join. The conclusion returns the slope to the interior and restates the alternative without which the preceding pages are only a more eloquent form of despair.

A word about tone, since tone is how a book is misfiled. I am not writing as a prophet and not as a comedian, though I will not pretend that the circus is always solemn. I am writing as a person inside the arrangement being described, which is the only honest location available. The first person is not a claim of special suffering. It is a refusal of the fake aerial view from which a corporate-state hybrid prefers its critics to speak, as if they were consultants. Consultants are in Chapter 4. This is not that.

A word, also, about the reader I am not trying to convert in a night. Conversion in a night is the feed's product. A political critique that has taken Camus seriously cannot treat a reader as a soul to be harvested for a cause. The hope, if a book of this kind is allowed a hope, is smaller and more adult: that a person who has felt the dual ledger in the wage, the classroom, the camera, the ballot, and the war naming, might recognize the ledger as one machine rather than as five unrelated disappointments, and might find the tradition named above a less humiliating response than the coma.

A word about how to read the chapters without turning them into five pamphlets. They are sequential because the machine is sequential. Extraction produces a class. The class is taught and watched. The taught and watched are offered a ballot that has already been filtered. The filtered public is then asked to underwrite a slope overseas. Skipping to Chapter 5 because the proper nouns are current is the feed's habit, and it will reproduce the feed's conclusion: a jersey, a certainty, a local justification. Starting at Chapter 1 and staying through the domestic rooms is the only way the flashpoint can be seen as a flashpoint rather than as a team sport. The conclusion is not an appendix. It is the place the diagnosis is required to become an alternative, or else the book is only Glubb's Age of Intellect in paperback form, brilliant and filed.

I should say, too, what I mean by fantasy in the list of sources above, since a light drizzle of it can sound like a dodge. I mean the stories an empire tells itself about its own eternity, the stories a market tells itself about its own freedom, and the stories a security state tells itself about its own necessity. Those stories are not empirical claims. They are atmospheres. A book that refuses to breathe them will sound, to a reader still inside them, like a person describing water to a fish. The drizzle is there to name the water. It is not there to replace the documents, the testimony, or the arithmetic.

The two-hundred-and-fiftieth year is a civic carnival as much as it is a date. There will be merch. There will be speeches that treat 1776 as a costume drama in which the present population has been cast, without audition, as the grateful descendants. There will be very little mention of the East India Company, of the dual ledger, of Glubb, or of the fact that a republic which cannot tell a shareholder from a citizen is not practicing the preamble it prints on the souvenirs. This book is dated to the carnival on purpose. Occasion is a kind of leverage. A reader who would not pick up a critique of corporate rule in an ordinary June might pick one up in the June that is being sold as eternity. I am willing to use the sale. I am not willing to honor it.

The table of contents, restated as a map rather than as a list:


Chapter 1. 250-Year Empire Theory. Late state capitalism. Glubb's six stages, the founding extraction, and the hybrid that kept the market's vocabulary after discarding its discipline.

Chapter 2. Inevitable Proletarian Revolution. The Industrial Revolution and its consequences. Autonomy against complexity, structural violence, the workday as absurdity, the gig costume of the old relation.

Chapter 3. Illusion of Education and Surveillance Capitalism. The panopticon, the police, ICE, and the figure Orwell popularized and Snowden documented. Worthy victims begin here, as a domestic filter, before they travel.

Chapter 4. Puppet Politicians, Freedom, and Domestic Affairs. The big club. Duopoly as supply chain, attention as a budget, the ballot as a valve.

Chapter 5. Foreign Policy and the Precipice of WWIII. Iran and Israel as flashpoint, not brief. Chomsky's primacy, Herman and Chomsky's victims, Snowden's overseas ledger, Camus's slope, Glubb's overextension.

Conclusion. The Age of Decadence read backward through those rooms, and the anarcho-syndicalist alternative restated as councils, federation, and a peaceful dissolution of corporate rule in favor of We The People.

If the coma patient would like to remain awake, the next page is Chapter 1. If not, the ruins will still be there. They are, as Paglia said, already littering the earth, and they were, in every case Glubb counted, produced by people who had been told they were the exception.
