# Chapter 4: Puppet Politicians, Freedom, and Domestic Affairs

There is a comedian's line that has done more to describe American politics than most political science departments manage in a semester: it's a big club, and you ain't in it. I will not reproduce George Carlin's routine here. The routine is his, the observation is older than he was, and most readers already feel its truth every time they watch two parties campaign on opposite platforms and legislate on the same donors. This chapter is the mechanism behind the feeling. I want "puppet politicians" to stop being a slogan and become a description of how a person gets on a ballot, what they are permitted to do once they arrive, and why the word *freedom*, in the speeches that get them there, so rarely extends to the wage, the lease, or the hospital bill.

Chapter 3 argued that a public can be educated into consenting to its own watching. This chapter argues that the same public is then invited, every two or four years, to perform a ceremony of choice inside a range that was narrowed long before the ceremony began. The ceremony is not nothing. I will not pretend that administrations are identical, or that the difference between them has no cost in human lives. The argument is narrower and, I think, more damning: the range of outcomes on the table in any given election is itself the product of a filtering process, so that voters are asked to choose energetically between options that were screened for them well before the ballot was printed. A population can exercise its full civic enthusiasm within that narrowed range and still never touch the questions that were excluded from it.

> The club does not need a locked door. It only needs dues too high for most of the country to pay.

I write this as a peaceful structural critique, in the same register as the rest of the book. Naming a club is not a call to storm it. It is a refusal to keep calling the club a marketplace, a family, or a vehicle of popular will, when the evidence, including evidence gathered by people with no sympathy for the tradition this book argues from, says otherwise.

## The Club Without a Locked Door

A club, in the ordinary sense, is a membership organization with rules about who may enter, who may speak, and who may set the terms of the evening. The American political class is a club in that sense even when it performs openness. The performance is not trivial. Primaries are held. Debates are televised. Volunteers knock on doors. A person with no famous last name can, in theory, file to run. The theory is the civics illusion of Chapter 3 applied to the ballot. The practice is a set of thresholds (money, party infrastructure, media attention, donor comfort, the willingness of a consultant class to attach its reputation to a name) that do the work a locked door would do, without the embarrassment of a lock.

I am not describing a smoke-filled room in which a dozen men assign offices over brandy, though rooms of that vintage still exist and still matter. I am describing a selection environment. Organisms that cannot survive in an environment do not appear in it, and the ones that do appear look as if they were designed for it, because in a sense they were. The environment of viable candidacy in the United States selects for people who can raise large sums from a small number of sources, who will not threaten the sources, and who can narrate that arrangement as service. The narration is often sincere. Sincerity is not a disproof of selection. A person can honestly believe they are fighting for ordinary people while having been filtered, at every prior stage of their career, by ordinary people's opposite.

The dues are not a membership card. They are the cost of being heard. Television time, digital advertising, staff, legal compliance, the travel that makes a national campaign look national: these are not optional ornaments. They are the price of appearing real to a media system that treats money raised as a proxy for seriousness. A candidate who cannot meet the price is not banned. They are covered, if they are covered at all, as a curiosity, a protest, a spoiler, a lesson in how the system allows dissent and then, politely, does not let it sit at the table. The civics textbook calls this a marketplace of ideas.

> A marketplace in which the stall fee exceeds the annual income of most of the customers is not a marketplace. It is a club with a gift shop.

None of this requires a conspiracy in the cinematic sense, no room of men lighting cigars over a global plan. It requires only that a system reward the behavior that sustains it and starve the behavior that threatens it, the same evolutionary logic that shaped the industrial machine in Chapter 2. The club does not meet annually to decide the starving. The starving happens in the ordinary course of consultants advising clients not to waste money on a candidate who cannot win, of reporters declining to cover a candidate who has not raised enough to be "viable," of voters, exhausted, declining to study a name they have never seen on television. Each of those decisions is locally rational. Their sum is a membership list.

I want the reader to notice how much of the club's power is borrowed from the education system of the previous chapter. A public taught that the two parties are the natural form of American disagreement will experience a third option as unnatural, even when the third option is closer to the public's stated preferences than either of the two. A public taught that voting is the consummation of citizenship will experience any critique of the ballot's range as a kind of impiety. The club benefits from that piety. It did not have to invent it. The classroom installed it, the newsroom maintains it, and the campaign consultant invoices for it.

## A Marketplace With Two Sellers

The two-party structure is often defended as a marketplace of ideas, but a marketplace with exactly two sellers is not a marketplace. It is a duopoly, and duopolies do not compete on substance. They compete on branding while agreeing on supply. The agreement is not always written. It appears as a set of questions that never reach the floor, a set of donors who give to both sides, a set of industries that are regulated, in practice, by their own alumni, and a foreign policy whose range, as Chapter 5 will argue, is narrower than the domestic slogans would predict.

Duopoly is a word from economics, and I am using it that way. In a competitive market, a seller who ignores the customer loses the customer to a rival. In a duopoly, the two sellers can ignore a great deal of what the customer wants, so long as they keep the customer choosing between them rather than leaving the market. American electoral politics has become very good at making leaving the market look like a moral failure. The nonvoter is lectured. The third-party voter is lectured more severely. The lecture is not always wrong in its particulars: spoiler effects are real, first-past-the-post voting punishes defection, and a vote for a candidate who cannot win is, in a narrow sense, a vote withheld from a candidate who might. The lecture is wrong in what it refuses to notice. It treats the electoral system as weather, a condition to which citizens must adapt, rather than as a designed constraint that the club has every interest in preserving.

I have watched, in more than one cycle, the same play performed with different costumes. In the months before a presidential nomination is settled, there is a brief interval in which a candidate who talks about money in politics, or about the downward ownership of the economy, is treated as a story. The story has a shape: insurgent, authentic, unlikely. Then the money consolidates, the superdelegates or the endorsements or the remaining calendar do their work, and the insurgent is invited to give a speech at the convention in which they praise the nominee they ran against. The speech is framed as unity. It is also a demonstration, to anyone watching, of what happens to a challenge that is absorbed rather than answered. Absorption is cheaper than persuasion. The duopoly has made an art of it.

Branding, in this arrangement, does an extraordinary amount of work. One party is permitted to own the language of markets and order. The other is permitted to own the language of care and inclusion. Each language is real enough to mobilize a base and shallow enough not to threaten the donors who fund both languages. A voter who cares about the language will experience the choice as existential. A donor who cares about the underlying allocation of property, tax incidence, enforcement priorities, and the permission structure around capital will experience the choice as a matter of tone. The voter is not stupid. The voter has been given a menu on which the items that would change the allocation are not printed. Choosing passionately among the printed items is what the ceremony is for.

## Campaign Finance as the Real Primary

Campaign finance is the supply chain here. A candidate who needs tens of millions of dollars to be viable is, by construction, a candidate who has already been screened by the people who can write checks of that size. The voter's choice happens after the real filtering has already occurred. Call the public election the general. Call the donor's decision the primary that does not appear on a calendar. The second primary is earlier, quieter, and more decisive. It does not require a conspiracy. It requires a bank account and a sense of which names are safe.

I want to be specific about what "screened" means, because the word can sound like a blackball in a velvet room. Sometimes it is that. More often it is a sequence of ordinary professional judgments. A bundler decides whether to host a dinner. A political action committee decides whether to reserve airtime. A party committee decides whether to share its voter file. A newspaper's political desk decides whether a candidacy is "serious," and seriousness is measured, circularly, by the presence of the dinners, the airtime, and the file. At no point does anyone have to say: we are excluding the public's preferred option. They only have to say: this one cannot win. The prophecy fulfills itself, and the civics teacher calls the fulfillment democracy.

The legal architecture around this sequence has a history. The post-Watergate reforms of the 1970s were a genuine attempt to constrain the role of money, and they were, in their time, a concession that the previous arrangement had been a scandal. The subsequent decades were a lesson in how a club absorbs a reform. Contribution limits were paired with the rise of vehicles that were not, technically, contributions. Parties professionalized their fundraising. Independent expenditures, after a series of court decisions, became a parallel campaign that the candidate could not coordinate with and therefore could not be held responsible for, a theological distinction that fools no one who has watched an advertisement and then watched the candidate smile. The public was told, at each stage, that speech was being protected. Some of it was. The speech of a person who can buy a network of advertisements is not the same object as the speech of a person who can hold a sign. Treating them as equal, as a matter of constitutional doctrine, is one of the club's more successful recodings, comparable to the recoding of data purchase as a market transaction in Chapter 3.

A worked example, small and ordinary, because the large scandals are the ones the system knows how to isolate. A city council race in a mid-sized American city. The seat pays little. The cost of a competitive campaign, once mail and digital and a staffer are included, exceeds the salary. The people who run are people who can afford to, or people who have been promised that the donors will make it possible. The donors are developers, a hospital system, a police union, a teachers' union, a handful of professionals who want a relationship with whoever wins. None of this is illegal. None of it is even, in the local paper's language, particularly interesting. The council then votes on zoning, on a hospital tax exemption, on a police budget, on a charter-school expansion. The votes are not bought in the crude sense. They are prepared, months earlier, by the fact that the people in the seats have already practiced being intelligible to the people in the rooms where the money was. Intelligibility is the soft form of capture. It does not require a bribe. It requires a social world.

## Puppet Politicians as Mechanism

"Puppet" is a rude word, and I am keeping it because the polite words have failed. A puppet, in the literal sense, is an object whose motions are produced by a force the audience is not supposed to watch. The American elected official is not an object. They have a will, a biography, often a conscience, sometimes a genuine desire to reduce harm. The puppetry is not in the absence of will. It is in the enclosure of will inside a set of constraints that make certain motions almost impossible and certain other motions almost automatic. The hand is not a single oligarch. The hand is the environment I have been describing: donors, party, consultants, media incentives, the permanent staff of agencies, the threat of a primary, the promise of a board seat afterward.

I have known, at a distance, people who went to Washington or to a statehouse with the intention of being un-puppeted. Some of them passed a bill I was glad existed. All of them learned, faster than they expected, the difference between a vote they were allowed to advertise and a vote that would end the dinners. The learning is not always cynical. It is often described, by the people who have done it, as growing up, as discovering how things actually work, as the death of naivete. That description should be taken seriously. It is the club's pedagogy. The freshman arrives with a mandate. The senior leaves with a skill set: how to raise, how to message, how to survive. The mandate is what the public thought it was buying. The skill set is what the institution actually produces.

The afterlife of office is part of the mechanism. A committee chair who becomes a lobbyist, a regulator who becomes a vice president of the industry they regulated, a staffer who rotates from the Hill to a firm and back: these are not secret. They are on LinkedIn. They are the ordinary labor market of the club. A person inside that market does not have to betray a principle in a dramatic hour. They have to remain employable in a world whose employers are the people the principle would have constrained. Employability is a discipline. Foucault, in the previous chapter, described discipline as the production of a subject who watches themselves. The revolving door is a panopticon for ambition. You do not need to be told, in a memo, which industries are not to be seriously injured. You need only imagine your next job.

I want to distinguish this from the claim that every politician is corrupt in the sense that they take bags of cash. Some are. Most do not need to be. A system that can produce compliant outcomes from non-corrupt individuals is a more impressive system than a system that requires corruption, and it is a harder system to campaign against, because the campaign against it sounds, to a public raised on the civics illusion, like an accusation against decent people. I am not accusing decent people of secret villainy. I am describing the enclosure of their decency. Decency that cannot touch the allocation of property, the structure of enforcement, or the permission of capital is decency as branding. It is not nothing. It is not power.

The anarcho-syndicalist implication is, again, about who holds the machine. A political system owned downward (candidates selected by wealth, legislation drafted with industry, agencies staffed from industry, careers completed in industry) will not become a vehicle of popular self-government because the vehicle has a "D" or an "R" on the bumper. The bumper is the branding. The drivetrain is the club.

## Juvenal's Grain and the Modern Circus

The Roman satirist Juvenal had a name for the mechanism that keeps a disenfranchised population from noticing, or acting on, its disenfranchisement: *panem et circenses*, bread and circuses. His complaint, in the tenth satire, was that a citizenry which had once traded its votes for real political consequence (the power to confer commands, fasces, legions, everything) had been quieted instead with free grain and gladiatorial spectacle, until the population stopped asking for power altogether and asked only for the next distribution and the next show. The genius of the arrangement, if it can be called that, is that it requires no censorship and no violence to work. It requires only that the spectacle be reliably more immediately gratifying than the slow, uncertain work of political organizing, and it will win that competition almost every time.

I am not claiming that Americans are uniquely stupid, or that Rome is a perfect analogy, or that a person who watches a game is a dupe. I am claiming that a political economy which has every interest in minimizing popular interference in its allocations will invest, heavily and rationally, in the alternatives to interference. The alternatives in Juvenal's Rome were grain and the arena. The alternatives now are a consumer economy engineered to make the next purchase feel like the resolution of whatever tension the day produced, and a media environment engineered to occupy the hours in which a person might otherwise have noticed that the allocations were not made with them in mind.

The grain is not metaphorical. A substantial portion of the American population is food-insecure in a country that produces caloric abundance, which is Peter Joseph's structural violence in its most literal form, and a substantial portion of the remainder is fed by a chain of processed products whose cheapness is a political achievement, a farm-bill and logistics achievement, as much as an agricultural one. Cheap calories are not a circus. They are a stabilizer. A population that can eat, even badly, is a population that will not, as a rule, treat the food system as a site of revolt. Chapter 1's sugar, the 126 grams, belongs here as well as there. The Sugar Act was a tax on a pleasure. The modern food environment is a flood of the same pleasure, subsidized, advertised, and medically costly, which performs a related function: it occupies the body, the budget, and the attention of people who might otherwise have occupied the club.

The circus is easier to see and easier to sneer at, which is why I want to be careful. Sport, streaming, the infinite feed, the outrage cycle that is itself a form of entertainment: these are not simply distractions invented by a villain. They are also some of the only remaining common culture a stratified country has. A person who works a job they do not control, in a city they cannot afford, under a medical debt they cannot discharge, is not a fool for wanting two hours in which none of that is the subject. The political question is not whether spectacle should exist. The political question is what happens to a republic when spectacle's share of the attention budget crowds out the share that would have been spent on the allocations the spectacle is, among its other functions, there to obscure.

Juvenal was not a radical in the tradition this book belongs to. He was a conservative satirist watching a civic capacity rot. I am willing to steal the diagnosis from a man I would not steal the politics from, because the diagnosis does not care who states it. A people that has traded the power to confer commands for the right to be fed and amused has not been conquered in the cinematic sense. They have been managed. Management is the club's preferred verb. It sounds like care. It functions like enclosure.

## The Attention Budget: A = P + S + O

The modern version of bread and circuses does not hand out grain in a public square. It streams a nearly infinite feed of entertainment, sport, and outrage directly into a device most people carry within arm's reach at all hours, alongside a consumer economy engineered to make the next purchase feel like the resolution of whatever tension the feed produced. It is worth stating this as a budget constraint rather than a metaphor, because it is one.

Let *A* denote a person's total daily attention, a genuinely finite resource, allocated across political engagement *P*, spectacle consumption *S*, and everything else *O*, such that

> *A* = *P* + *S* + *O*

Because *A* is fixed, any increase in *S* must come at the expense of *P* or *O*, and a system with a strong interest in minimizing *P* has every incentive to maximize the availability, novelty, and immediate reward of *S*, since *dP/dS* < 0 whenever *A* is held constant. A population scrolling a feed engineered for maximum engagement is not a population failing to care about the "big club" described earlier in this chapter. It is a population whose attention budget has been reallocated by design, one autoplay video and one algorithmic outrage cycle at a time, away from the one use of that attention the club has the most reason to fear.

I want the equation to do what the *p* versus *p̂* equation did in Chapter 3: make it harder to moralize a structural fact. A person with a depleted *P* is often described, by people with more *P* than they know what to do with, as apathetic, low-information, a disappointment to the franchise. Some people are apathetic. Many more are exhausted. Exhaustion is not an accident that happens to a budget. It is what a budget looks like when *S* and *O* (*O* including the second job, the commute, the child-care logistics, the medical paperwork, the unpaid work of being poor in a country that makes poverty a full-time administrative task) have already spent the day. Telling an exhausted person to "be more engaged" is like telling Bentham's inmate to stand somewhere the tower cannot see. The architecture is the subject. The inmate's character is the alibi.

The feed is particularly efficient at converting political feeling into spectacle, which is a more precise harm than mere distraction. A person who spends an hour angry at a clip is a person who has spent *P*-shaped emotion on an *S*-shaped object. The anger is real. The object is designed to be unfinishable: there is always another clip, another villain, another humiliation, another victory that lasts until the next refresh. Unfinishable objects are the dream of any system that wants feeling without organization. Organization requires a finished object, a meeting, a demand, a next step. The feed offers a substitute that feels like the next step and is, in fact, the postponement of it.

Outrage as circus deserves its own sentence. Juvenal's arena was honest about being a show. The contemporary arena is often dressed as information, as "staying informed," as the duty Chapter 3's civics class installed. A person who watches three hours of political programming, none of which leaves them with a lever they can pull before the next cycle, has not been informed in the sense that would threaten a club. They have been occupied. Occupation can look like citizenship from the inside. That is why it works.

*O*, the remainder, is not a residual in the dismissive sense. It is sleep, care, friendship, the maintenance of a body and a household, the work that makes *P* and *S* possible at all. A politics that treats *O* as sloth is a politics that has already sided with the people whose *O* is performed for them by someone else. The club's members have staff. The club's subjects have errands. Any honest accounting of the attention budget has to price the errands.

The anarcho-syndicalist tradition I named in the introduction has always understood this, even when it did not write it as an equation. A working day that leaves nothing for the meeting is not a personal failure of the worker. It is a design feature of a workday set by someone else. Shortening the part of life that is owned downward is not a lifestyle preference. It is a precondition of *P*. The eight-hour day was not won because people wanted more time to watch spectacles, though they used some of the time that way. It was won because people needed time to become a public. The feed is, among other things, a machine for buying that time back.

## A Worked Example: Tuesday Night

Let me spend a Tuesday with a person this book is about, not as a statistic and not as a saint. They get home, if home is the word, at 6:40, having left at 7:10 in the morning. Dinner is a sequence of decisions that do not feel like politics and are: what is cheap, what will the child eat, what will not spoil before Friday. The phone is on the counter because the phone is how the school announces a closure, how the boss announces a shift, how the family announces a crisis. The phone is also, by the time the dishes are in the sink, a hole through which *S* arrives without being asked. A clip of a politician saying something outrageous. A clip of a comedian saying the first clip was outrageous. A score, a trailer, a face. The person is not a fool. They know, at some level, that the club is real. They also have to be awake at 6:00. *P*, tonight, is a notification from an app that voting is in November, which it is not, and a headline they do not open.

Now put, on the same Tuesday, a fundraiser in a hotel ballroom. The food is better. The *A* in the room has been allocated, for years, toward *P* of a particular kind: the *P* of being in rooms where allocations are discussed as if they were weather one might usefully influence. No one in the ballroom has to be a villain. They are doing what the environment rewards. They will leave with a photograph and a sense of having participated in democracy. The person at the sink will leave Tuesday with the dishes done. Both will be told, in November, that they had an equal say.

The inequality is not only money, though it is also money. It is the inequality of *A* that money buys. Staff buys *O*. A short commute buys *A*. A body that is not in pain buys *A*. A child with reliable care buys *A*. The club's members are not merely richer in dollars. They are richer in the resource the equation names, and they spend it on the one activity, *P*, that the rest of the budget's structure has made expensive for everyone else. When a pundit says the public is "checked out," I would like the pundit to publish their Tuesday.

I will add a third Tuesday, because two points make a line and three make a pattern. A staffer in a legislative office, twenty-six, talented, underpaid by the standards of the firms that will later hire them, spends the evening calling people who gave last cycle to ask whether they will give this cycle. The staffer's *P* is almost entirely this. They went into the work because they cared about a housing bill. The housing bill is not what the call list is for. The call list is for the environment that made the boss viable. The staffer is not a puppet in the rude sense. They are a person whose Tuesday has been enclosed. In five years they may be in the hotel. In ten they may be writing the language that makes the housing bill safe for the people they used to call. The enclosure does not require a conversion experience. It requires Tuesdays.

## The Ballot as Pressure Valve

Kaczynski's argument about autonomy, discussed in Chapter 2 in the context of industrial technology, applies here as well, in a way he himself gestured toward: political autonomy erodes under the same pressure as personal autonomy, because both depend on an individual's ability to meaningfully affect the systems governing their life. A citizen who votes every two or four years for one of two donor-vetted candidates has exactly as much control over domestic policy as a factory worker has over the assembly line's speed, which is to say, control mostly in the form of a complaint mechanism rather than an actual lever.

> The ballot becomes a pressure valve rather than a steering wheel, useful for letting off frustration, rarely useful for changing direction.

I want the valve image to be exact. A pressure valve is a real device. It prevents an explosion. It does not steer the machine. A public that can vote out a particular face, and cannot vote out a particular allocation, has been given a valve. The face changes. The allocation, on the questions the club cares about (the permission structure around capital, the enforcement structure around labor and migration, the tax structure around wealth, the surveillance structure of Chapter 3) remains within a band the club can live with. The public experiences the face-change as power, because the civics illusion taught them that the face is the system. The club experiences the face-change as weather. Weather is survivable. Steering would not be.

None of this means elections do not matter. They matter enormously at the margins, and the difference between administrations has real consequences for real people, which is exactly why it would be dishonest to wave the whole system away as theater in the simplistic sense. Who sits on a labor board, who is appointed to a civil-rights division, who is named to a court, who is deported and who is not: these are not decorations. They are lives. A book that shrugged at them in order to sound radical would be a book that had decided some lives were theoretical. I have not decided that. The argument here is that the range of those lives' possible official futures has been narrowed upstream of the vote, and that celebrating the vote without inspecting the narrowing is the piety the club prefers.

A valve also teaches a lesson, which is part of its political function. People who pull the lever and do not see the allocation change learn, over cycles, that pulling the lever is what there is. Some become cynical, which the club can tolerate, because cynicism does not organize. Some become more partisan, which the club can also tolerate, because partisanship inside a duopoly is branding. A few become organizers, which the club tolerates less well, and which Chapter 3's apparatus is, among its other functions, there to watch. The valve does not only release pressure. It trains the public in what pressure is allowed to look like.

I have stood in lines to vote. I will stand in them again. I am not asking the reader to abandon the valve. I am asking the reader not to confuse it with a wheel. A person who treats the valve as the whole of politics will have their frustration released on a schedule the club can set, in a building the club can decorate, under rules the club can live with. A person who treats the valve as one device among others (organizing at work, organizing in a neighborhood, refusing the story that the only adult act is a ballot) is a person the club has always found inconvenient. Inconvenience, not purity, is the relevant political virtue.

## Malcolm X and the Vote That Is Assumed

Malcolm X saw this clearly enough to build an entire speech around it. In "The Ballot or the Bullet," his argument was not that voting is worthless. It was that voting without leverage is theater, and that a community which shows up every four years to choose between two versions of the same indifference should not be surprised when both versions govern the same way. He was speaking to Black Americans specifically about a system that took their votes and returned nothing proportional to them, but the structural critique generalizes: any bloc of voters that can be assumed, rather than earned, will be taken for granted by the party assuming it and ignored by the party that has written it off. That is not a partisan observation. It applies with equal force regardless of which letter follows the candidate's name.

The speech is from 1964, which is worth keeping in view, because the subsequent sixty years are often narrated as the fulfillment of the ballot half of his title. The Voting Rights Act passed. Formal barriers fell, were rebuilt in new materials, fell again, were rebuilt again. Black Americans, and other communities that had been fenced out, became electorally essential in a set of states and cities. Essential is not the same as answered. A bloc that is essential to a coalition and has no credible exit from the coalition is a bloc that can be photographed at the rally and neglected in the allocation. Malcolm's point was not that the ballot was a trick in the sense that the machine was rigged to miscount, though miscounting and suppression have always been part of the American story. His point was that a ballot without an independent basis of power (organization, economic leverage, the credible capacity to withhold) is a ballot the club can afford to win or lose without altering its diet.

I will not flatten him into a mascot for this book's conclusions. He was a man in a specific struggle, under a specific surveillance (the file of Chapter 3), speaking to a specific audience about a specific history of broken promises. The generalization I am making is about structure, not about claiming his voice. The structure is this: a party that believes it owns a population's vote will behave like an owner. An owner does not have to be cruel every day. They only have to know that the asset cannot easily leave. The other party, believing the same population is unreachable, will not spend to reach it, and will spend instead on populations it might still buy. The assumed vote and the written-off vote are two halves of the same contempt. They produce, between them, the indifference Malcolm named.

The bullet in his title is a word I need to handle with the care this book's opening note requires. He was speaking, in context, about the right of self-defense and about the refusal to pretend that a closed door was a waiting room. I am not taking the word as an instruction, and I am not offering one. The usable remainder, for a peaceful structural critique, is the insistence that a politics with only a ballot is a politics the club has already priced in. Leverage lives elsewhere: in labor, in organized withdrawal of cooperation, in the building of institutions that do not ask the club's permission to exist. That is the syndicalist thread again, and it is why Malcolm belongs in this chapter as well as in the last. The file on him was a recognition, by the state, that a man teaching people to stop being an assumed vote was more dangerous to the arrangement than a man teaching people to vote harder.

A contemporary worked example, without pretending it has his stature. Consider a constituency that votes, cycle after cycle, at eighty percent for one party, in cities where that party controls the machinery of housing, policing, and schools, and where the housing is still unaffordable, the policing still extractive, and the schools still a function of zip code. The constituency is told, each cycle, that the other party would be worse. The telling is often true. Worse is not a policy. Worse is a hostage note. A politics that can only offer "worse" as its argument for loyalty is a politics that has already confessed it does not intend to pay what loyalty costs. The club can live on hostages. It cannot, indefinitely, live on hostages who have learned the word.

## Donor Preference Versus Voter Preference

"Domestic Affairs" in this chapter's title is doing quiet work. The same donor class that filters the candidates also lobbies the legislation those candidates eventually pass, so the loop closes: money selects the politician, the politician selects the policy, and the policy protects the conditions that generated the money in the first place. Freedom, in that loop, becomes a word reserved for the ballot box and rarely extended to the wage, the lease, or the hospital bill, the places where most people's actual unfreedom is experienced day to day.

Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page measured the mismatch directly, and I want to use their finding without inventing a page number I am not looking at. In research comparing the policy preferences of average citizens with those of affluent citizens and organized business groups, the pattern that emerges is blunt: when the public wants one thing and wealthy interests want another, the wealthy interests' preference is the one that, far more often, becomes law. Broad public support, on its own, is a weak predictor of whether a policy is adopted. Support among affluent actors and business organizations is a strong one. The finding has been public for years, has been debated in the usual ways, and has not, in its broad outline, been replaced by a finding that the median voter runs the country. A democratic user interface over an operating system that tracks donor preference is not a glitch. It is the product.

The usual objections are worth hearing. One is that the affluent and the public often agree, so the test of divergence does not apply to every issue. True, and unhelpful. The issues on which they agree are not the issues on which a club is defined. A club is defined by what it does when the membership's interest and the neighborhood's interest part ways: on tax structures, on labor law, on financial regulation, on enforcement against the powerful as opposed to enforcement against the poor. Another objection is that business groups sometimes lose. Also true. A system does not need to be omnipotent to be skewed. It needs to be skewed. A third objection is that voters are uninformed, and that the affluent are simply better at knowing what works. This is the civics illusion in a lab coat. It converts a power differential into an IQ differential, and it cannot explain why the "what works" so reliably works for the people who funded the study of what works.

I want a worked example that does not depend on a reader's prior partisanship. Take the gap, persistent across administrations, between public support for making it easier to join a union and the legislative success of making it easier to join a union. The public, in poll after poll over years, is more sympathetic to the idea than the congressional docket is. The donor class, particularly but not exclusively on the side that owns more of the country's private payrolls, is not. The docket follows the donor. A reader who likes unions will call this corruption. A reader who dislikes unions will call it a filter against bad policy. Both readers are describing, from opposite moral seats, the same operating system: donor preference as the load-bearing input, voter preference as a decorative one.

Take, as a second example, the durability of a tax structure in which income from work is treated less gently than income from ownership. The public's views here are mixed and manipulable, because the civics of tax is taught even more poorly than the civics of the Fourth Amendment. What is not mixed is the intensity of preference among the people who live on ownership. Intensity plus access beats mild, exhausted, poorly informed majority feeling. That is not a law of nature. It is a law of the rooms in which bills are written. The rooms are staffed by people whose Tuesdays, as above, are allocated toward the intense.

Peter Joseph's concept of structural violence, introduced in Chapter 2 in the context of economic scarcity, extends naturally into this political sphere. A political system that produces policy outcomes matching donor interest far more reliably than voter preference is not failing to represent the public by accident, any more than a factory failing to pay a living wage is failing by accident. Both are the system working as designed, just not as advertised.

> That is not a marginal distortion of democracy. It is a different operating system running under a democratic user interface.

## Domestic Affairs Downstream of Money

Domestic affairs, in the political-journalism sense, is a folder: health care, housing, education, crime, immigration, the budget. In the sense this chapter needs, domestic affairs is the set of allocations that determine whether a life is livable in the country the civics class described. The folder is not governed by the public that lives inside it. It is governed, predominantly, by the people who fund the governors. Downstream is the right word. Water does not argue with gravity. Policy does not, on the load-bearing questions, argue with the primary that happened before the primary.

Health care is the clearest current. A public that lives with medical bankruptcy, with insulin prices, with surprise bills, with a maze that turns illness into paperwork, is a public with a preference that has been measured and remeasured: they would like this to hurt less. The hurt continues, in configurations that change enough to supply campaign contrast and not enough to threaten the industry's existence. Both parties have, at various times, offered reforms that the industry could survive. Survival of the industry is the constraint. Relief of the public is the branding. A person who has argued with an insurer on a Tuesday evening, while the ballroom raised money, already knows which of those is load-bearing. They may not have the sentence for it. They have the hold music.

Housing is a close second. A country that treats shelter as an asset class will produce, as its ordinary output, a population that is one illness or one layoff away from a car. Zoning, interest rates, tax treatment of ownership, the political power of existing owners to prevent supply that would reduce the value of what they hold: these are domestic affairs. They are also a map of who is in the club. A first-time buyer competing with an investment fund is not failing at the American dream. They are arriving at a market the dream's earlier beneficiaries have enclosed. The enclosure is legal. The civics class called it the economy.

Immigration policy, which Chapter 3 already touched as an enforcement and data-broker problem, belongs here as domestic affairs because it is among the most useful tools the duopoly has for branding. One party performs severity. The other performs compassion. The underlying allocation (a labor market that wants the work and a legal system that withholds the status, an enforcement apparatus that is expensive and a pathway to belonging that is slow) serves employers and the political need for an enemy more reliably than it serves the people moving or the people already here. The data-brokered, partnership-enforced apparatus of the previous chapter is the machinery. The branding of this chapter is the paint. A person in a mixed-status household is not watching two philosophies contend. They are watching a club manage a workforce and a base at the same time.

Crime and policing, likewise. The public's fear is real. The public's experience of being over-policed or under-protected is also real, and it is not evenly distributed. The club's interest is in an enforcement capacity that can be pointed, as Chapter 3 argued, at populations that are politically cheap to point at, and in a rhetoric that can be sold to populations that are electorally expensive to disappoint. Reform arrives as a press conference. Capacity accumulates as a contract. The contract is domestic affairs. The press conference is the circus.

Education (the subject of the last chapter, returning as a budget line) is funded in a way that makes a child's resources a function of their neighbors' property values, which is a remarkably efficient device for transmitting the club's membership into the next generation while reciting, in the classroom, that talent and grit are what matter. A civics course that teaches equality of opportunity inside a building whose roof depends on the local tax base is a course in a contradiction the student is not given the math to name. The math is domestic affairs. The recitation is the pledge.

## Structural Violence in the Legislature

I want to take Joseph's term out of the factory and set it down on the floor of a chamber. Structural violence, as he uses it, is harm produced by an arrangement rather than by a blow. A legislature that can spend a year on a symbolic bill and cannot spend a week on a housing bill that would reduce homelessness is not a legislature that failed to find the time. Time, again, is a budget. The budget was allocated. The allocation produced, as its output, a certain number of people remaining on the street. No member had to hate those people. Hatred is not required for the harm. The harm is the residue of the rooms the members could not afford, in the campaign-finance sense of afford, to disappoint.

This is why the puppet image, rude as it is, is more accurate than the corruption image for most of what goes wrong. Corruption, in the public mind, is a bag of cash and a hidden account. It is prosecutable, which is why it is useful as a spectacle: a member is led away, the chamber congratulates itself on self-cleaning, the allocation remains. Structural violence is not prosecutable in that way. No statute forbids a chamber from spending its year on the bills the donors can live with. No inspector general is assigned to the absence of a bill. The absence is the product. Product is the right word. A factory that does not make a thing it was never tooled to make is not malfunctioning.

I am aware that this will be read, by some, as a claim that legislation never helps anyone. That is not the claim. Unemployment insurance helps. Medicaid helps. A minimum-wage increase, when it survives the rooms, helps. Help that must survive those rooms will tend to be help that does not reallocate power. A higher wage inside a still-unowned workplace is not nothing. It is not the same as the workplace being governed by the people who work it. The club can tolerate the first, up to a point, and has, historically, tolerated it when the alternative appeared to be the second. The New Deal was, among other things, a price paid to avoid a different ownership structure. I am not sneering at the price. I am noticing that it was a price, paid under pressure, and that the pressure, not the piety, was the independent variable.

## Freedom, Narrowed

Freedom, in the campaign speeches, is a large word. It is the flag, the franchise, the right to speak, the right to worship, the right to own, the right to be left alone. Some of those rights are real and were paid for in struggles this book has no interest in diminishing. The narrowing I am after is the narrowing of which freedoms are treated as political facts and which are treated as personal problems.

The freedom to speak is a political fact. The unfreedom of a person who will be fired for using it, at work, about work, is a personal problem. The freedom to vote is a political fact. The unfreedom of a person whose ballot cannot touch the lease is a personal problem. The freedom to move is a political fact. The unfreedom of a person whose movement is a data trail sold to an agency, as in Chapter 3, is a privacy setting. Personal problems, in the club's dialect, are not what politics is for. Politics is for the large word. The large word is then cited as evidence that the personal problems cannot be that serious. A free person, by definition, would not have them. The definition is doing a great deal of work.

I am not inventing a new theory of liberty. I am noticing that the version installed in the classroom and recited at the convention is a version that stops at the property line of the club. Inside the line, freedom is thick: to deploy capital, to write a rule, to enforce, to surveil, to exit a market, to fund a candidate. Outside the line, freedom is thin: to choose among the candidates, to speak without much hope of being consequential, to work for a wage set elsewhere, to rent from an owner whose freedom is the thick kind. A language that uses the same word for both is a language that has already taken a side.

The narrowed version of freedom is also a management tool for *P*. If freedom is already complete, then further politics is ingratitude, or it is the special pleading of people who want something for nothing. The club is very good at reciting the completeness. It has the classroom, the anthem, the anniversary: two hundred and fifty years, as I write, of a story that began with slaveholders talking about liberty, a beginning this book's preface declined to launder. The anniversary will be a circus of a particularly solemn kind. Solemn circuses are still circuses. They occupy *A*. They spend it on feeling rather than on inspection.

I do not want a smaller freedom. I want the word to be expensive again: to cost, if it is going to be used, an actual capacity to shape the conditions of a life. Capacity is measurable. It looks like a lease you can afford, a clinic you can enter without a prior authorization ritual, a workplace you have some jurisdiction over, a file you can inspect, a ballot that can touch the file, the clinic, the lease, and the workplace. In the absence of those, the word is branding. Branding is what puppets are for.

## What Was Never on the Ballot

I will end this chapter the way I ended the last, with the assignment the public is given and the assignment it is not. The assignment is to choose. The choice is real within its range. The range was the subject of these pages. What was never on the ballot, in most of the cycles a reader will remember, was whether campaign finance should function as a private primary; whether a lobbyist should draft the language that regulates their former employer; whether a data broker should be permitted to sell a life to an agency that would have needed a warrant to collect it; whether a workplace should be governed by the people who spend their waking lives inside it; whether the feed should be allowed to run a deficit against *P* without being named as a political actor; whether the club should exist in this form at all.

Those questions are called unrealistic when they appear, which is how a club talks about questions it has not priced in. Unrealistic is a word that sounds like a description of the world. It is a description of the membership. A thing is realistic when the people with dues can live with it. A thing is unrealistic when they cannot. The job of a book like this is not to make the second kind of thing sound easy. It is to stop calling the first kind of thing the limit of the possible.

Domestic affairs, in the end, are downstream of the same dynamic driving every other chapter in this book: a system that has learned to reward the behaviors that sustain its current arrangement and to make the behaviors that would threaten it feel unrealistic, fringe, or naive. The "big club" survives not because its members are uniquely corrupt individuals, though some certainly are, but because the structure selects for compliance at every rung of the ladder before a person ever reaches a position to change it. Freedom, understood as the capacity to meaningfully shape the conditions of one's own life, turns out to be scarcer inside that club than the campaign speeches suggest, and scarcest of all for the people who were never invited to apply for membership in the first place.

The next chapter turns the same lens outward. An empire that has enclosed its domestic politics inside a club, and its domestic population inside a panopticon, does not, as a rule, become generous or wise abroad. It becomes, in Glubb's sequence, overextended, certain of its necessity, and increasingly unable to tell a war of interest from a war of survival. Iran and Israel will be the names on the map. The machine that decides which names become necessities will be the subject. I mention it here only to keep the reader from imagining that the club stops at the water. The club does not stop at the water. The water is one of the things the club owns.

I will not close with an instruction. I will close with a refusal. I refuse to call a duopoly a marketplace. I refuse to call a donor primary a conversation with the people. I refuse to call a valve a wheel. I refuse to call a Tuesday at the sink apathy, or a Tuesday in the ballroom civic virtue, without asking what *A* was spent on and who was paid to spend it. I refuse to treat Carlin's observation as a joke that exhausted its truth when the set ended. The set ended. The club did not. And I refuse, as this book's opening note required, to confuse that refusal with a permission to do harm. Harm is the club's ordinary output. Naming it is not the same as joining it.
