# Chapter 2: Inevitable Proletarian Revolution

## The Whistle Replaces the Sun

If Chapter 1 traced the birth of the American empire, this chapter traces the engine that fed its growth. The Industrial Revolution is usually taught as an unambiguous triumph: the steam engine, the assembly line, the leap in output that supposedly lifted humanity out of subsistence. What tends to be left out of that story is the wholesale re-engineering of the human being into a component of production. The farmer who once measured his day by the sun was replaced by the factory hand who measured his by the whistle. That shift in the unit of time is, in miniature, the whole argument of this chapter.

I want the reader to feel the shift as a bodily fact, not as a chapter heading. A day governed by light has slack in it. Weather delays the harvest. A tool breaks and you sit in the shade while you mend it. A child is born and the work rearranges itself around the fact. None of this is Eden. Peasant time could be brutal, hungry, and short. I am not selling a costume-drama countryside. I am naming a difference in who owned the clock. When the unit of time becomes the whistle, the clock has a proprietor. The proprietor does not live by the sun. The proprietor lives by throughput. Your fatigue is not a season. It is a variance to be managed. Your need to piss is not a fact of being an animal. It is a deduction from the hour.

The early mills made this crude and therefore honest. Bells, then steam whistles, then time clocks you punched so that a clerk you never met could reconstruct your day as a row of numbers. Taylor would later give the method a graduate degree, breaking motions into units, timing them with a stopwatch, and calling the result science. Ford would give it a line, a wage high enough to buy the product, and a speed that made the wage feel like a bargain you were lucky to keep. The costume changes. The relation does not. Someone else owns the interval in which your body is allowed to be a body.

Glubb's Age of Commerce, named in Chapter 1, is not only a story about railroads and tariffs. It is a story about this theft of the interval. Territory had been taken. Territory had to be made to pay. The industrial system is how land, coal, cotton, and iron were converted into a claim on other people's hours. Chomsky's later language of profit over people is, in one sense, only the mature voice of the whistle. The whistle says: your life, for this stretch, is an input. The mature system says the same thing with a human-resources vocabulary and a wellness app.

I am not predicting a guillotine in this chapter, and I am not issuing instructions. I am doing what the title requires and what the author's note already limited: a political critique of an arrangement that cannot, by its own arithmetic, keep purchasing human time at a discount forever. The word "inevitable" in the title is not a date and not a method. It is a claim about stress. A class that owns nothing but its capacity to work, placed against a class that owns the means of turning that work into a return, will produce a tension that compounds. Whether the outlet of that tension is reform, rupture, resignation, or some federated construction I will argue for later is the open question of the book. The tension itself is not optional. It is the load-bearing structure.

## What the Schoolbook Leaves Out

The schoolbook version of industrialization is a parade of inventions. Watt improves the engine. Arkwright organizes the mill. Whitney's gin, Fulton's boat, the locomotive, the telegraph, Bessemer steel, Edison's shop, the moving assembly line in Highland Park in 1913. Each item is real. Each item is also a way of not talking about the people whose days were re-cut to fit the item. Output per worker rose. So did the number of hours a child could be asked to spend in a room full of belts. So did the number of ways a grown person could lose a hand, a lung, a year, a spine.

Enclosure in Britain is the prologue the American story prefers to skip, because it makes the "free laborer" look less like a volunteer. Common land, imperfect and often already stratified, was fenced, titled, and made exclusive. People who had mixed subsistence with small sale were invited, at the point of a statute, to become the market's idea of a person: someone with nothing to sell but time. The United States had its own versions, less often called enclosure. The closing of the frontier, the making of land into grants for railroads that dwarfed what a homesteader could actually claim, the convict lease, the company town, the agricultural ladder that dumped tenants into mills: a series of invitations to show up at a gate with a body and no other ticket.

Lowell, Massachusetts, is useful because it was designed as a showcase. Farm daughters in boarding houses, moral supervision, a literary magazine, a wage that looked, for a short window, like a path. The window closed. Speeds rose. Wages stuck or fell. The daughters were replaced, in large part, by immigrants who could be paid less for the same whistle. The showcase had demonstrated something the owners would spend the next two centuries denying: that the industrial gift was always a bargain with an expiration date written in someone else's office. Manchester, England, had already shown the un-showcased version, the one Engels walked through and that American boosters treated as a European disease. It was not a disease. It was the system without makeup.

Child labor is the detail American memory has agreed to treat as solved, which is a way of not asking what principle was ever rejected. The principle was never that a child's body is not an input. The principle, once statutes arrived, was that a child's body is an input with a minimum age and a maximum hour, subject to exemptions in harvest and in family firms and in the present's "internship." The adult body remained, and remains, fully available. I am not reciting this to produce outrage, which is Stage 5's favorite inexpensive emotion. I am reciting it because the proletarian condition this chapter is about does not begin with a smartphone. It begins when survival is mediated through a wage that the waged did not set, in a workplace the waged do not own, under a clock the waged do not control.

The Civil War, in the commercial stage I sketched in Chapter 1, is also an industrial event. Northern mills wanted a tariff and a labor market that was not competing with unpaid chattel in quite the same way. Southern planters wanted cotton to remain a social order and not only a crop. The emancipated population was then offered, in too many places, a new enclosure: Black Codes, vagrancy statutes, convict labor, sharecropping that reconstituted a claim on bodies under a different letterhead. An industrial history of the United States that starts with steel and skips this reconstitution is a history written for the owners of the letterhead. The whistle did not abolish unfreedom. It offered unfreedom a new metronome.

## A Diagnosis I Will Not Inherit Whole

Theodore Kaczynski's manifesto, *Industrial Society and Its Future*, is easy to dismiss because of the man who wrote it, and it should be dismissed as a guide to anything a decent person might do. I will be more specific than that, because this book named him in the introduction and I will not have a prosecutor, or a fan, pretend the naming was coy. He killed people. He maimed people. He mailed bombs. He wrote a text that treats atrocity as an instrument. I reject his violence without remainder, without a poetic clause, without a "but his ideas" that is allowed to smuggle the instrument back in. I will not reproduce the manifesto. I will not outline a campaign. I will not so much as sketch a target or a device. A reader looking for a how-to is in the wrong book, and I want that reader to leave now.

I raise the underlying diagnosis anyway, with that fence around it, because the diagnosis does not belong to him in the way a method would, and because pretending the diagnosis is radioactive has not made industrial life more humane. His claim, stated without his numbering and without his prescriptions, was that industrial-technological systems, once established, cannot be reformed piecemeal in a way that restores individual autonomy, because the system requires that autonomy to shrink as the system's complexity grows. A person cannot opt out of dependency on the electrical grid, the food-distribution network, or the wage economy any more than a cell can opt out of the organism it belongs to. I raise this not to endorse his methods, which I reject outright, but because the underlying diagnosis, that industrial complexity purchases convenience with autonomy, is one the reader should sit with before dismissing the whole problem on the reputation of one author.

Kaczynski trained as a mathematician before he became anything else, and the text he produced borrows the shape of a proof more than the shape of an essay: propositions building on prior propositions rather than paragraphs building on rhetoric. That shape is part of why the diagnosis travels, and part of why it is dangerous in the hands of people who think a proof about a trend line is a warrant for a crime. It is worth stating the load-bearing claim in that language rather than diluting it into mood, and then stating, in ordinary language, that a trend line is not a license.

I want a second fence, not only around violence but around nostalgia. The diagnosis can be misread as a hymn to a cabin. I do not want a cabin as a national model, and I said so in Chapter 1. A clinic is complexity. A grain elevator is complexity. A sewage plant is complexity. I would like those to exist. The question this chapter is actually asking is not whether complexity should be burned. It is who owns it, who is permitted to be ignorant of it because someone else is forced to tend it, and whether a person can live a consequential life without kneeling to the ownership. That is a syndicalist question. It is not a murderer's question. If the two get braided in a reader's mind, the braid was not mine.

Peter Joseph, whose work I trust far more as a moral starting point, will enter in a moment from the other direction: not technology as such, but the economic structure technology got bolted onto. Camus will enter as a description of what repetitive labor does to a mind that is still a mind. Marx will enter as a description I will not follow into a state. The Kaczynski material is here only as a compact statement of a cost function that the industrial age made obvious and that late state capitalism has made nearly inescapable. Compact is not the same as complete, and complete is not the same as permitted.

## Complexity, Autonomy, and the One-Way Function

Let C denote a system's technological and administrative complexity, and let A denote the practical autonomy available to an individual embedded in that system: the degree to which a person can meet their own needs, solve their own problems, and make consequential decisions without depending on a chain of specialists and institutions they do not control. The core claim can be written as a simple monotonic relationship:

> dA/dC < 0

As complexity rises, autonomy falls, not because any single actor intends it, but because complexity by definition distributes the competence needed to run a system across an ever-growing number of specialized roles, none of which any one person can fully occupy or opt out of. You may be a superb nurse and still be unable to repair the imaging machine, grow the food in the cafeteria, write the software that stores the chart, or alter the staffing ratio that is burning you out. Each of those tasks is someone else's slice of C. Your A, in the hours you are on the floor, is the slice you are allowed. The rest of the organism is elsewhere, and it does not ask you.

This is easy to caricature as a complaint about expertise. It is not. I want experts. I want the person who knows the reactor not to be me. The autonomy at issue is not omniscience. It is the capacity to withdraw, to refuse, to reconfigure a life, without the organism treating your refusal as a system failure that must be corrected. A peasant could, in a bad year, still be a peasant. A nurse who walks away from the staffing ratio walks away from a credential, an insurance network, a city whose rents assume the credential, and a patient who did not create the ratio. The moral weight of staying is real. So is the fact that the weight was arranged. Complexity that cannot be refused without hurting someone who did not design it is complexity that has been weaponized, even if no one in the room thinks of themselves as holding a weapon.

The reason this behaves like a one-way ratchet rather than a reversible dial is that dismantling complexity carries its own cost, call it R, the reversal cost, and R itself scales with C rather than staying constant. The more deeply a system is embedded in food distribution, medicine, energy, and communication, the more catastrophic it becomes to unwind, even if unwinding it would eventually restore the autonomy A measures. R(C) grows faster than any population's tolerance for the disruption reversal would cause, which is why societies almost never choose to reduce complexity voluntarily. They have it reduced for them, by collapse, once R(C) exceeds what the system can absorb.

That sentence is the one a certain kind of reader has been waiting for, the reader who wants collapse as a cleansing. I am not that reader and this is not that invitation. A fast, forced R is a famine with extra steps. It is also, historically, a gift to whoever still holds stocks of food, fuel, and armed employees. Collapse does not return autonomy to the nurse. Collapse returns power to whoever stored canned privilege. The asymmetry between a slow, chosen A and a fast, forced R is the real argument, more than any specific technology singled out in a text I will not quote. It is also exactly why I keep separating the diagnosis from the treatment a murderer chose for it.

> A correct description of a one-way function does not, by itself, license a reader to skip to the ending he chose. The math describes a trend line. It does not authorize a method.

I want that distinction on the page as plainly as the equation is. dA/dC < 0 is a claim about a direction. R(C) is a claim about why the direction is sticky. Neither claim tells you to hurt a stranger. Neither claim tells you that the grid should be dropped. What they tell you, if you are reading them as a syndicalist rather than as a spectator of apocalypse, is that any politics which leaves C in the hands of a class that profits from a low A will keep finding reasons why your refusal is unrealistic. The blackmail of reversal cost is how late state capitalism talks when it is frightened. "You need us" is the polite form. "Without us the kitchen crushes" is the form I used in Chapter 1. Both forms are sometimes empirically true in the short run, which is why they work. The long-run answer is not to prove them false by smashing the kitchen. It is to make them false by teaching the kitchen to run without that particular landlord.

## Why a Description Is Not a Method

I am going to spend more words than a stylish chapter would spend on this fence, because the name in the introduction is a name that attracts two kinds of bad readers: the one who wants a saint of solitude, and the one who wants a pretext to call every industrial critic a bomber. Neither will get what they want from me.

A description of a trap is not an instruction to shoot the trapper, especially when the "trapper" is a dispersed set of ownership relations and the people in range of a violent fantasy are clerks, graduate students, and bystanders. Violence against persons does not reduce C. It does not raise A for anyone who has to live in the aftermath. It increases the state's brief for surveillance, which Chapter 3 will take up, and it hands the owners of C a morality play in which they are the injured party. If you want a practical reason to reject the method, not only a moral one, there it is: it strengthens the arrangement it pretends to attack. The moral reason is simpler and sufficient. People are not levers.

I also reject the quieter cousin of that method, the hope that if enough systems fail at once, autonomy will grow in the cracks. The dA/dC relation is about a functioning complex system. A failing complex system is not a simple society. It is a complex system with shortages, and shortages are a sales territory for warlords, firms, and whatever remains of a state. Peter Joseph's term, structural violence, is useful here in advance of the next section: a collapse does not end the production of unnecessary suffering. It changes the logo on the suffering. Camus, who had actually seen a Europe in ruins, did not treat rubble as a philosophy. He treated it as a demand to rebuild without lying.

So what is left of the diagnosis once the method is refused? Something I think is still hard, and still useful. Hard: you will not recover pioneer-style autonomy inside a fully industrial order by adding a privacy setting or a right-to-repair sticker, not because those fights are worthless, but because they do not reverse the derivative. Useful: once you stop expecting the derivative to reverse itself, you can stop wasting a life on the kind of reform that only modernizes the whistle. You can ask a different question, the syndicalist one. If C cannot be wished away, who can hold it without holding it as a weapon? People who already operate it, federated, accountable sideways to one another rather than upward to a share price, are the only candidates I find plausible. That is construction. It is slow. It does not photograph well. It is the opposite of a mailed device.

I will not pretend this construction is guaranteed. "Inevitable" in my title attaches to the tension, not to the quality of our response. A society can live with a negative dA/dC for a long time, numbing it with syrup, screens, and the small dignities of a paycheck, until Glubb's sixth stage has finished the job by other means. The diagnosis explains the numbness. It does not excuse it, and it does not deputize anyone to cure it by atrocity. If I have not said this enough times, it is because some sentences need to be unskippable.

## Peter Joseph and the Manufacture of Need

Peter Joseph makes a related argument from the other direction in *The New Human Rights Movement*. Where the diagnosis I have just fenced located a problem in the behavior of technological systems as such, Joseph locates it in the economic structure that technology got bolted onto. He calls it structural violence: not the visible violence of a police baton, but the invisible violence of a system that produces scarcity in the midst of abundance because scarcity is profitable. The factory did not just industrialize labor. It industrialized need. Advertising, planned obsolescence, and the wage relation itself all manufacture a hunger that the system is uniquely positioned to sell the cure for.

I want that word, manufacture, to keep its factory smell. A hunger that occurs because a harvest failed is one kind of fact. A hunger that occurs because a firm has an interest in the next model, the next flavor, the next subscription, the next twelve-hour availability of your attention, is another. The second hunger can coexist with warehouses full of goods, which is the obscenity Joseph is naming. We are not, in the aggregate, a poor species. We are a species running an accounting system that requires pockets of deprivation the way an engine requires compression. Without the pocket, the price does not hold. Without the price, the claim on other people's hours does not hold. The whistle, in this light, is not only a way to extract time. It is a way to make sure you must return tomorrow because today's wage was calibrated to yesterday's rents, and yesterday's rents were calibrated to the idea that you will return.

Advertising is the lyric department of this calibration. I am not above being advertised to; nobody who has lived in this country is. The point is not purity. The point is function. A person whose autonomy has already been reduced by C, who cannot easily meet needs outside the wage, is then subjected to a constant education in new needs, so that the wage which already does not cover the old ones can be experienced as a personal failure rather than a design. Planned obsolescence is the hardware department of the same education. A phone that could have lasted is retired by software. A garment that could have been mended is built not to be. A tractor that a farmer might have repaired is copyrighted in its guts. Each instance can be defended as innovation. The pattern is a tollbooth on durability, which is to say a tollbooth on A, because a durable tool is a little island of autonomy and islands are bad for throughput.

Joseph is sometimes waved away as a man from the internet with a Zeitgeist problem, the way any critic who talks to a camera is waved away by people who prefer their radicals dead and bound in cloth. I am interested in the part of his argument that is older than his films. Johan Galtung's structural violence, the slow harm that never needs a villain in the room, is the ancestor. The industrial contribution is only this: once production is abundant enough that deprivation has to be arranged, the arranging becomes a full-time professional activity. Late state capitalism, as I defined it in Chapter 1, is among other things the state-backed version of that profession. Farm bills that overproduce corn and then require a bloodstream to absorb the syrup. Housing kept scarce as an asset class in cities full of empty units. Medical billing that turns a healed body into an ongoing revenue stream. The scarcity is not a drought. It is a business model.

Put Joseph next to the dA/dC claim and the shape of the proletarian condition becomes clearer than either offers alone. Complexity reduces the practical ability to opt out. The economic structure then sells you the products that make staying feel like a life. You are not only unable to leave the organism. You are talked into calling the organism a menu. That is a more advanced unfreedom than the mill bell, which at least had the decency to sound like a command.

## The Description Without the Prescription

The Industrial Revolution did not simply create wealth. It created a class whose labor produced that wealth without producing a proportional claim on it. Marx is the obvious name absent from my list of influences, and I have left him out deliberately, because the argument here does not require his solution, only his description of the problem: a class that owns nothing but its own capacity to work will always be structurally positioned against a class that owns the means of turning that work into profit. That tension does not resolve itself. It compounds, generation over generation, until the aggregate stress finds an outlet.

I want to be exact about the refusal of the solution. A party that seizes the state in the name of the proletariat has, in the historical cases that actually occurred, tended to become a new proprietor of C, with a new police, a new production quota, and a new reason why your A must wait. That is not a television talking point I swallowed. It is a record. Anarcho-syndicalism is, among other things, a wager that the problem was never only which flag flies over the ministry of production. The problem is ministry as ownership. Workers' councils, federated, revocable, boring in the way that actual meetings are boring, are a different architecture. They can fail. They have failed. They have also, in stretches of Spain in the 1930s, in shop-floor experiments that never got a state, in cooperatives that still exist and get treated as cute, demonstrated that the people who do the work are not a mysticism. They are a fact the share price needs and therefore cannot admit is competent.

Whether the outlet of the compounding tension is reform or rupture is the open question of this book. I am not predicting a guillotine. I am pointing out that a system built on an ever-widening gap between the value labor creates and the value labor receives is, by its own arithmetic, unstable. Stage 1 of Glubb's empire cycle gave us pioneers willing to work for a stake in the outcome. Two hundred and fifty years later, the outcome has been claimed by shareholders who never touched the machine, and the workers who do touch it are told to be grateful for the wage.

The gap can be measured in more than slogans. Productivity and typical pay, which I already named as a 1970s uncoupling in the Age of Affluence, is one instrument. The share of national income captured as profits versus wages is another. The hours a household must sell to afford a median shelter is a third, and in the cities where C is densest that third instrument has become a cruel joke. None of these require a conversion experience. They require an adult to look at a chart without immediately asking who is to blame among the poor. The compounding is the point. A gap that widens is not a mood. It is a physical stress on a social material. Materials fail at joints. The joints in this system are the places where people still have to look at one another: the warehouse at shift change, the hospital hallway, the apartment stairwell, the group chat that starts as jokes and becomes a list.

I should also say what I am not doing with Marx's description. I am not dividing the country into a saintly factory floor and a demonic office. Plenty of waged people in offices are, in the relevant sense, proletarian: they own little but their trained time, they can be dismissed, their output is captured above them. Plenty of small proprietors are one medical bill from the same condition. The useful sharpness of the old vocabulary is not a census. It is a relation: if your survival is a residual after someone else's return, you are in the relation, whatever your job title says. Gig platforms understood this well enough to spend millions insisting their drivers are entrepreneurs. We will get to the app. The insistence is the tell.

## Sisyphus Clocked In

Albert Camus, though I place him more heavily in Chapter 5, wrote something in *The Myth of Sisyphus* that belongs here as well. He describes the workman's life, the same movements repeated day after day toward a fate that never truly changes, as a kind of absurd condition, and he asks what it would mean to recognize that absurdity honestly rather than numb yourself to it. The assembly line did not invent repetitive, meaningless labor, but it industrialized it, standardized it, and then handed it a paycheck just large enough to make the repetition tolerable.

> The workman of today works every day in his life at the same tasks, and this fate is no less absurd. But it is tragic only at the rare moments when it becomes conscious.

Camus was not writing an economic critique. He was writing a philosophical one. The two overlap more than either discipline likes to admit. A system that structures a person's most waking hours around motion without meaning is going to produce either revolt or resignation, and a great deal of modern labor policy is built around managing which of the two shows up. The paycheck is the management. So is the story that the motion is a ladder. So is the syrup in Chapter 1, the short burst that makes the next identical hour imaginable. So is the entertainment that Joseph would recognize as a manufactured hunger filling the hours the wage does not, and that Chapter 4 will treat as the modern bread and circus.

Camus wanted, famously, to imagine Sisyphus happy: a consciousness that takes the rock as its own and in that taking refuses the gods. I have always thought the line was both brave and, in a factory key, incomplete. Sisyphus in the myth is punished by gods. The modern workman is punished by an ownership structure that can replace him with a man from the next town, or with a machine that does not need to imagine anything. Happiness in the Camus sense, a lucid fidelity to the rock, is a spiritual achievement I will not mock. It is also, if it becomes the official advice to a working class, a lullaby sung by people who own other rocks. Lucidity that cannot become organization is Stage 5 again: brilliant inner life, whistle still on time.

Resignation is the more common output, and I do not despise it. People have children. People are tired. People have learned, correctly, that a scene in a break room can cost a rent. Labor policy, union avoidance as a profession, at-will employment, the scheduling algorithm that punishes the person who said no once: these are machines for producing resignation at scale. Revolt, when it appears, is treated as a disruption of services, which is an interesting phrase. Services to whom. Disruption of what. The rock paused, for a moment, and the gods filed an injunction.

I am not telling anyone to become Camus's rebel in a way that gets them fired for a metaphor. I am saying that the industrial age made the absurd a mass condition, and that a mass condition will not stay forever in the private skull. Either it becomes a politics or it becomes a symptom: the addictions, the rage, the shootings that American commentary is always willing to psychologize and never willing to clock. The whistle taught a country to treat human time as identical units. Identical units that can still feel are a contradiction with a long fuse. The fuse is not my invention. It is the title of the chapter, misunderstood if you think I lit it.

## The App Is a Factory

The twenty-first-century version of this machine no longer looks like a factory floor. It looks like an app that dispatches a driver, a warehouse that times a worker's bathroom breaks by algorithm, a call center that scores empathy the way a factory once scored units per hour. The costume has changed. The underlying relation has not: a worker's time is purchased at a rate the worker did not set, in a market the worker does not control, to produce value the worker will not retain a proportional share of.

Gig-economy platforms describe their workers as independent contractors and entrepreneurs, a rebranding that manages to combine the flexibility of the old cottage industry with the surveillance of the modern factory, since the algorithm tracking a driver's route and rating is a supervisor that never clocks out. Flexibility is the cheaper tea of this arrangement. You may log on when you like. You may also discover that when you like does not coincide with when the surge is, that the car is yours to repair, that the insurance is yours to carry, that the idle hour is yours to eat, and that a customer with a thumb can end a week's food. Risk, as Chomsky framed it for banks, is here socialized downward onto the person least able to carry it, while the platform retains the part of the return that comes from being the choke point.

I want a worked example, not a vibe. A driver accepts a ride. The price was not bargained. It was issued. The route is suggested with a force that is not quite a command and not quite a suggestion. The time to arrival is scored. The conversation is, in effect, scored. The acceptance rate is scored. Fall below a threshold and the "independence" reveals itself as a gate. None of this requires a foreman in a rumpled shirt. C has been concentrated into code, which is cheaper than a shirt and does not join a union. A, for the driver, is the freedom to choose which twelve-hour stretch to be available for a system that will not disclose how the price was made. That is a very small A sold at a very large markup of rhetoric.

The same structure appears in delivery, in task work, in the "creator" economy that tells a person they are a media company while a platform sets the terms of being seen. Each case is defended as novel, a disruption, a way for students and retirees to smooth income. Some people do smooth income. The design is not a smoothing cooperative. The design is a firm that has learned to own the dispatch without owning the liabilities that used to come with employees. Late state capitalism, again: privatize the profitable coordination, socialize the crash, the hospital bill, the missing wage, onto a person classified out of the twentieth century's incomplete protections.

If you hear in this the dA/dC relation, you are hearing correctly. The app is more C than the old dispatcher with a paper map: more optimization, more data, more integration with maps, payments, ratings, insurance gray markets. The driver's A is not increased by the sophistication. The driver cannot bargain with a function. The driver cannot even see the function. Autonomy falls as the dispatch becomes more capable. Convenience rises for the person who summons the car. That person is often, themselves, late for a whistle. The system is not a conspiracy of riders against drivers. It is a waterfall of extracted intervals, each level taking a cut of the level below, terminating in a share price that does not know anyone's name.

## The Warehouse, the Call Center, and the Algorithmic Whistle

Amazon's warehouses, and the many quieter warehouses that imitate them, are the factory without the courtesy of calling themselves a mill. A body is a pick rate. A scanner is a time clock with a location. Walking becomes a metric. Standing still becomes a metric. Injury becomes a metric that is managed as a cost, which is not the same as being prevented as a horror. Bathroom breaks timed by algorithm are the detail that leaks into newspapers because the public still has a residual sense that pissing is not supposed to be a performance review. The residual is useful. It is also revealing. We are shocked by the bladder and unsurprised by the rest of the captured day. The bladder is the playing card of this system, the small taxed thing that makes the whole tax visible.

Call centers, many of them far from the customer and some of them in the same cities that lost the mills, score tone, script adherence, average handling time, hold time, after-call work, and a sentiment that a model claims to detect in the voice. Empathy becomes units per hour. A person is asked to produce the feeling of being cared for at a speed that makes care impossible, then punished by a dashboard when the impossibility shows. This is Camus with a headset. It is also Joseph: the need being manufactured is the customer's need to be soothed by a human voice, cheaply, so that the firm can operate a machine that is not quite a machine and not quite a staff. The worker's A is the script. The rest is C owned elsewhere.

Healthcare, logistics, food service, education adjuncting, retail: I can multiply settings until the chapter becomes a catalog, and catalogs are how Stage 5 pretends to have acted. The settings rhyme. A schedule issued by software. A rate set elsewhere. A monitoring that would have required, in 1913, a foreman on a catwalk and now requires a badge, a camera, a login, a GPS ping. Taylor's stopwatch was expensive in labor. The current stopwatch is a byproduct of devices people pay for the privilege of carrying. dA/dC is not an abstraction in these rooms. It is the reason a person can be excellent at a job and still have no say in the job's speed.

Unions, where they still have breath, are trying to bargain with this, and they should, and this book is not too sophisticated to say that a contract is better than a prayer. A contract does not reverse the derivative. It can raise the wage, slow the rate, put a human in between the algorithm and the bladder. Those are real goods. They are also, if they are the end of imagination, a modernization of the whistle rather than a change in who owns the mill. I will take the modernization over the current cruelty. I will not confuse it with abolishment. Abolishment, again, is not a smashed warehouse. It is a warehouse whose purpose and tempo are set by the people who already know how to run it, federated with the drivers and the clerks and the nurses, rather than by a fund that knows how to run a slide deck.

The algorithmic whistle has one feature the steam whistle lacked: it can pretend not to be a command. Steam was paternal. Code is procedural. "The system" denied the hours, "the system" deactivated the account, "the system" flagged the rate. A procedure without an author is the dream of every proprietor who would like the benefits of being obeyed without the inconvenience of being seen. Snowden's world, in the next chapter, is this dream applied to a country. Here it is enough to say that a boss who is a procedure has not abolished the boss. He has hidden the office.

## Efficiency Is an Extraction Rate

What the diagnosis of complexity and Joseph's diagnosis of structured scarcity both point toward, from their very different starting points, is that this is not a series of unrelated inconveniences to be legislated away one gig-worker classification lawsuit at a time. It is the load-bearing structure of an economy built to convert human time into shareholder return as efficiently as possible, and every technological leap since the steam engine has made that conversion more efficient, not less.

Efficiency, in this context, is not a neutral word. It describes exactly how much of a worker's day can be extracted before the worker either organizes, breaks down, or is quietly replaced by a machine that does not need a lunch break or a union. An efficient warehouse is not one that injures fewer people if injuries are cheaper than slowing down. An efficient call center is not one that solves problems if solving problems lengthens the call. An efficient hospital is not one that heals if a bed empty for healing is a bed not billed. Once the maximand is return, efficiency is a synonym for the rate at which A can be driven down without igniting the outlet this chapter is named for.

I am not against all uses of the word. An efficient sewage plant is a moral good. An efficient vaccine line is a moral good. Notice, though, that those efficiencies are measured in outcomes people need. The efficiency of the whistle is measured in the residual after wages. Confusing the two is the ideological accomplishment of the Age of Commerce, still running. "You don't want to go back to cholera" is a serious sentence. It is also, in the mouths of people defending a pick rate, a stolen seriousness. The sewage plant does not require a billionaire. The pick rate does.

Replacement by machine sits at the end of the efficiency sentence as both threat and destiny. Automation is real, uneven, and endlessly announced. Where it arrives, it does not automatically free anyone. It transfers C upward and leaves the displaced to auction their remaining A in a glutted market of time. Where it does not arrive, the announcement still works as a disciplinary story: be grateful the whistle is still for you. A politics that treats automation as fate is a politics that has accepted dA/dC as a god. A politics that treats automation as a tool whose owners can be changed is a politics that still has a use for engineers who do not work for the fund. I would like those engineers. I would like them in the federation, not as priests.

Break down is the middle term I will not skip. Bodies fail. Minds fail. The American commentariat prefers to discuss this as a healthcare puzzle or an opioid puzzle or a loneliness puzzle, each puzzle with a donor-friendly task force. The workplace is often the mill that ground the puzzle pieces. A person who has been a pick rate for a decade is not a separate civic mystery when they go home and cannot sleep. Camus's consciousness, the tragic moment, is sometimes just pain. Joseph's structural violence is sometimes just a spine. If this sounds sentimental, the sentiment is a correction to a vocabulary that can say "human capital" without vomiting.

Organize is the first term, and the only one that is a politics rather than a medical chart. I will not write a manual. I will say that the industrial age, which invented the conditions, also invented the forms: the shop committee, the strike that is a folding of arms and not a fantasy of rubble, the mutual aid that keeps a kitchen open while a contract is fought for, the federation that keeps a single mill from being starved alone. Those forms are not nostalgia if the app is a factory. They are the appropriate technology for a factory that fits in a pocket. The algorithm cannot join. That is a feature. It is also a reminder that the opposite of C is not chaos. The opposite of privately owned C is socially held competence.

## The Arithmetic of an Unstable Gap

The proletarian revolution of this chapter's title is not a prediction of a specific date on a calendar. It is an observation that a system optimized this precisely for extraction cannot indefinitely coexist with a population that has enough information, enough shared grievance, and enough platforms to organize on to notice the pattern. History does not run on a fixed schedule, but it does appear to run on a fixed logic, and the logic here has not changed since the Sugar Act: tax a population's daily necessities long enough, dress up the extraction in official language long enough, and eventually the population reads through the language.

The gap is the tax. It is levied in rent, in medical bills, in student debt, in unpaid overtime, in the unpaid hour of waiting for the app to ping, in the metabolic bill of the syrup that makes the levy survivable for another shift. Official language calls the levy a market. Official language called Townshend an external duty. Literacy is the capacity to see a price as a relation. The colonists, for all their sins, had that literacy about tea. We are trained out of it about nearly everything else, which is the educational problem of Chapter 3, but the training is not perfect. People can still add. They can add a wage and a rent and a childcare hour and a commute and a body that hurts. When the sum does not work, they do not need my book. They need the sum not to work in public, with other people whose sums also do not work.

Instability, in this arithmetic, does not mean fireworks. It can mean a slow degradation of consent: declining trust, declining birth rates among people who can do math about nurseries, declining willingness to fight the empire's wars, declining belief that the photograph of 1965 is coming back. Glubb would recognize the degradation as late-stage weather. Chomsky would recognize the frantic attempt to manage it with stories. Joseph would recognize the new products that sell relief. Camus would recognize the temptation to call the whole thing absurd and be done. All of them, if I may seat them at a table they did not ask to share, would recognize that a gap which cannot be justified except by force or by trance is a gap that has already begun to fail. Force is Chapter 3 and Chapter 5. Trance is Chapter 3 and Chapter 4. This chapter is only the gap.

I do not need the gap to produce a cinematic uprising in order to call the current arrangement a failure. Failure is already distributed: in the life expectancy of the counties that kept the plants and lost the unions, in the warehouse spine, in the driver who owns a car the platform is consuming, in the nurse who loved the work and hates the staffing ratio. Revolution, as I am using it, is the name for the moment those distributed failures refuse to stay distributed. They find each other. They become a public. Whether that public then builds a federation or is harvested by a demagogue or a brand is not written. The inevitability is the finding, given enough time and enough remaining literacy. The rest is character, which no theory gets to skip.

A note on platforms, since I just named them as part of the condition for noticing. The same apps that dispatch the driver also let the driver talk, leak a dashboard, film a warehouse, map a wage. C is not only the owners' tool. That fact does not make C neutral. It makes the monopoly on narrative leakier than it was for a mill hand in 1890. Late state capitalism can live with leaks. It has a whole industry of managing them. But leaks are not nothing. They are a small rise in A around information, even as A around material life continues to fall. A politics that knows this will use the leak to federate and will not mistake the leak for liberation. A feed is not a union. A union with a feed is, at least, less alone.

## Organization Without a Boss

Anarcho-syndicalism, named in the introduction, is the positive content I will put against both the murderer's counsel and the shareholder's blackmail. Production organized by the people doing the producing. Federation upward through voluntary unions rather than ownership downward through shareholders. The state apparatus that props up the modern corporation treated as no more legitimate, and no more permanent, than the corporation itself. I will say again what the author's note said, because this is the chapter in which a certain kind of reader looks for a hidden knife. The following is not a violent piece and not a call to action in the operational sense. It is a political critique. Abolishment means dissolution of corporate rule, a peaceful means to put power back into the hands of we the people.

What does that look like in an industrial landscape rather than in a pamphlet? It looks, first, like taking seriously the competence that already exists. The driver already knows the city. The warehouse worker already knows the flow. The nurse already knows the ward. The engineer already knows the code that times the bladder. The present arrangement is not based on their ignorance. It is based on a legal fact: their knowledge does not include a claim. Change the claim and C does not vanish. C changes hands. Hands is the correct vulgar word. Shareholders do not have hands on the machine. That is the whole trick of distant ownership, the Age of Commerce's gift to the Age of Affluence.

I am not describing a weekend. Legal orders that took a century to build will not be wished into a cooperative at a meeting. I am describing a direction of construction that does not require a vanguard party and does not require a collapse. Cooperatives that scale, unions that bargain for control and not only for a raise, municipal ownership where a natural monopoly is already a fact, open knowledge where a copyright is currently a tollbooth on repair: these are boring sentences. Boring is a virtue after a century of exciting disasters. The Spanish CNT, before a civil war that I will not romanticize and that no American reader should try to reenact, showed that large networks of work could be coordinated without a board of absentee owners. The IWW, in this country, showed that a language of One Big Union could cross crafts the way the app now crosses them from above. History is not a cookbook. It is a proof of possibility against the blackmail that says possibility is childish.

dA/dC, in this architecture, does not magically become positive. A nurse in a federated hospital still cannot personally run the grid. What can change is the political meaning of that dependence. Dependence on a peer federation is not the same relation as dependence on a fund. You can argue with a peer. You can recall a delegate. You cannot recall a share price. Autonomy, in the sense of A, includes the ability to make consequential decisions. A federation that can vote a staffing ratio is an increase in A even if C remains high. That is the point the cabin-romantic misses and the reason I will not follow a diagnosis into a fantasy of simplicity. We can live with complexity if we can live with each other as the owners of it. We cannot live with complexity as a weapon forever without the weapon going off, which is not a metaphor I mean as a plan. I mean it as Glubb: stages end.

Peaceful is the word I will keep using even when it sounds like a prayer, because the alternative has a body count and a surveillance dividend I will not pay. Strikes, boycotts, dual power in the old sense of building the replacement in the shell of the present, legislation that is actually a surrender of corporate legal privileges rather than a new pamphlet of ethics: these are conflict. Conflict is not the same as atrocity. The colonists dumped tea. They also, later, did much worse, including to people who had no Parliament in London. I am not offering 1776 as a clean template. I am offering the literacy of 1773: the ability to recognize a company that has been written into the law as if it were a fact of nature, and to refuse the writing. Refusal can be a harbor. It can also be a union card, a cooperative charter, a municipal utility, a generation that stops treating the share price as weather.

## Inevitable Does Not Mean Blood

I close this chapter on the title, because titles are where misunderstandings go to dress up. Inevitable, here, is not fate in the astrological sense Chomsky already rejected for me in Chapter 1. It is arithmetic plus literacy. A gap that widens, a derivative that drives A down, a manufactured hunger that cannot quite numb everyone forever, a 250-year-old political culture that still contains, somewhere under the pageant, a memory of dumping a company's cargo: these do not add to a date. They add to a pressure. Pressure finds an outlet. I do not get to choose, in a book, that the outlet will be syndicalist and kind. I get to argue that it should be, and that the other famous outlets are traps.

Blood is the trap that pretends to be a shortcut through R(C). It does not unwind complexity. It militarizes it. It is the method I have rejected in the man I would not follow, and it is the method of states as well, which is a symmetry the man also missed. Resignation is the trap that pretends to be maturity. Camus knew the difference between lucidity and numbness, and numbness is what a dashboard is for. Demagogues are the trap that pretend to be the public the failures found when they found each other. Chapter 4 is that trap's electoral face. I am arguing for a fourth outlet: organization of the people who already run the machine, toward a dissolution of the legal order that says the machine is someone else's, without a romance of ruins.

If that is not revolutionary enough for a reader who came for a manifesto in the explosive sense, good. This is a manifesto in the older sense: a public statement of a position. The position is that the industrial re-engineering of human time, accelerated rather than cured by the app, has created a class relation that will not stabilize on the terms of late state capitalism, and that the adult response is to build a successor that can hold C without using it as a whip. The pioneers, at their best, were people who refused to fund an extraction they had not consented to. They then built an extraction of their own. We do not have to repeat the second half. We do have to decide whether we still know how to do the first: to read a price as a relation, to name a company when it has dressed itself as a market, to put power back into the hands of the people who do the producing, and to do it without becoming the thing we are describing.

The factory whistle was honest. It said the interval was no longer yours. The app is dishonest. It says you are an entrepreneur of your own exhaustion. Between those two sounds sits the whole of this chapter. I know which one I prefer as a teacher, and I know which one is louder now. Literacy is hearing the second as the first. Politics, of the only kind this book will endorse, is what you build after you have heard it, with other people who have heard it, in the long, unglamorous key of a kitchen that still has to feed everyone in the morning. The sun, for what it is worth, has not been replaced. It is still there. The whistle only trained us not to look up.
