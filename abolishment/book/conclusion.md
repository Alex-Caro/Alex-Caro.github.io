# Conclusion

The empire does not usually recognize the stage it is in while it is happening. It recognizes affluence, intellect, and its own historical greatness in a mirror it built for itself, and it mistakes the accumulation of all three for permanence. This book has traced how that mirror gets built: in the factory, the classroom, the surveillance file, the ballot box, and finally in a foreign conflict narrated as necessity. The argument is not that collapse is a date on a calendar. The argument is that a system which socializes risk, privatizes profit, and treats organized labor as a security problem is, by its own arithmetic, unstable.

I will say it once more, because the internet is a machine for misreading: this is not a call to violence. Abolishment, in the title, means dissolution of corporate rule over daily life: a peaceful transfer of power back to the people who already do the work. The ruins Glubb counted were not produced by pamphlets. They were produced by empires that could no longer tell the difference between their own continuation and the health of the people living inside them.

## Stage Six, Read Backward

Sir John Glubb's Age of Decadence is the name this book has been writing toward since the first page. It is not a moral insult aimed at a generation. It is a structural description of what happens when the wealth of the Age of Affluence, the self-consciousness of the Age of Intellect, and the outward posture of the Age of Conquests all continue after the interior sources of renewal have thinned. Chapter 5 located the foreign-policy expression of that thinning. The earlier chapters located the thinning itself. The conclusion's job is to read the stages backward, so that a reader who has been asked to look at a flashpoint is returned to the rooms in which the flashpoint was made possible.

Chapter 1 began with pioneers who fled a handful of taxes and founded a republic on a contradiction: slave owners who preached that all men are created equal. The Sugar Act, the Stamp Act, the Townshend Acts, and the Tea Act were not merely irritants. They were a structure: a distant authority extracting value from a population that had no seat at the table where the extraction was decided. The Tea Act was a bailout dressed as a tax cut, structured to prop up the East India Company by granting it a monopoly on colonial tea sales. Two hundred and fifty years later, the structure has not been retired. It has been domesticated. The favored corporation still writes rules for a market it also competes in. The public still absorbs the losses. The difference is that the extraction is now described as the free market, and the people being extracted from are invited, every two or four years, to ratify the arrangement by choosing among candidates the arrangement has already screened.

Late state capitalism, as Chapter 1 used the phrase, is what happens when the Age of Affluence refuses to end on schedule. Chomsky's *Profit Over People* named the revealed preference: risk socialized, profit privatized. That is not textbook capitalism. It is a state-corporate hybrid that kept the vocabulary of competition and quietly discarded the discipline of letting failure fail. A hybrid of that kind will, in stage six, defend itself with the language of security even when the thing being secured is the hybrid rather than the people living inside it. Chapter 5 was the demonstration. This conclusion is the reminder that the demonstration does not start at the water's edge.

Chapter 2 followed the engine. The Industrial Revolution re-engineered the human being into a component of production. The farmer's sun became the factory's whistle, and the whistle became the algorithm that times a warehouse bathroom break. Kaczynski's diagnosis, which I have used while rejecting his methods without remainder, was that complexity and autonomy move in opposite directions, and that the cost of reversing complexity grows faster than a population's willingness to pay it. Peter Joseph's diagnosis, from the other direction, was that the economic structure bolted onto that complexity produces scarcity in the midst of abundance because scarcity is profitable. Structural violence is the name he gave the result. Put those together and the proletarian condition is not a romantic costume. It is the condition of a class that produces value without a proportional claim on it, generation after generation, until the arithmetic becomes impossible to narrate as merit.

The proletarian revolution of that chapter's title was not a date and not a guillotine. It was an observation about instability. A system optimized for extraction cannot indefinitely coexist with a population that has enough information, enough shared grievance, and enough platforms to notice the pattern. History does not run on a fixed schedule. It does appear to run on a fixed logic, and the logic has not changed since the Sugar Act: tax a population's daily necessities long enough, dress the extraction in official language long enough, and eventually the population reads through the language. Stage six is what the empire does with that reading. It points the population at a foreign enemy, at a domestic spectacle, at a feed, at anything other than the ledger.

Chapter 3 described the installation of belief: that one is free, and that one is watched for one's own good. Chomsky and Herman's propaganda model explained the classroom and the news as upstream filters rather than as overt censors. Snowden's *Permanent Record* explained the watching as a default setting rather than as an exception. Foucault's panopticon, rebuilt in fiber, explained why the watching does not need to be total to be effective. It needs only to install a perceived probability of being observed close enough to certainty that the population does the rest of the work for free. Malcolm X's files, decades before the fiber, explained that the impulse is not a technology problem. Technology only made it cheaper. ICE, the police, the intelligence community, and the commercial data brokers are not four separate stories. They are one story about a state that treats organized populations it cannot predict as a security problem, and that describes the treatment as protection.

A public educated into deference and watched into self-censorship is a public that can be walked down Chapter 5's slope. The connection is not metaphorical. The same signals infrastructure that polices a browsing history informs a targeting decision. The same worthy/unworthy filter that decides which domestic life is a lesson decides which foreign death is a tragedy. The same classroom that treats COINTELPRO as a footnote will treat the next overseas campaign as a necessity. Stage five of Glubb's cycle produces the critique of all this. Stage six files the critique and funds the shipment.

Chapter 4 named the club. A duopoly is not a marketplace. Campaign finance is a supply chain. The voter's choice happens after the real filtering has already occurred. Juvenal's bread and circuses have been upgraded into a feed that reallocates a finite attention budget away from political engagement because political engagement is the one use of attention the club has reason to fear. Malcolm X's argument that voting without leverage is theater was never only about one community's ballots. It was about any bloc that can be assumed rather than earned. Peter Joseph's structural violence, applied to the legislature, is the finding that donor preference predicts policy and public preference does not. Freedom, in that loop, is a word reserved for the ballot box and rarely extended to the wage, the lease, or the hospital bill. Kaczynski's autonomy argument, applied to the same loop, is that a citizen with a complaint mechanism instead of a lever is not a citizen in the sense the speeches require. The ballot is a pressure valve. It is rarely a steering wheel.

Read those four chapters as the interior of stage six, and Chapter 5 stops looking like a change of subject. Overextension abroad is what a hollowed interior does with its remaining talent for posture. Primacy is what a club calls security. Worthy and unworthy victims are what a filtered public is given instead of faces. Signals intelligence is what a panopticon does when it travels. The arms economy is what a financialized industrial system does with the last sectors it still knows how to run. A flashpoint is what a slope looks like when the calendar says 2026 and the alliance commitments, the energy corridors, and the nuclear-adjacent files all occupy the same geography. Iran and Israel, I said there, are names on the mirror. They are not the cause of the mirror. The cause of the mirror is the arrangement Chapters 1 through 4 described, pointed outward at the moment the public has the least remaining capacity to stop the pointing.

## The Alternative, Named Without Romance

Anarcho-syndicalism, as I use it here, is not a romantic costume and not a vintage poster. It is the claim that production should be organized by the people doing the producing, federated upward through voluntary unions rather than owned downward through shareholders, and that the state apparatus propping up the modern corporation is no more legitimate, and no more permanent, than the corporation itself.

That sentence has been in this book since the introduction because the chapters would otherwise be only diagnosis, and diagnosis without an alternative is stage-five intellect: brilliant, true enough, and compatible with the next quarterly report. I want the alternative in ordinary language, because ordinary language is the language of the people who would have to live it.

A workers' council, in this usage, is a deliberative body of the people who do a particular kind of work, with the authority to set the terms of that work rather than to advise a boss who sets them. It is not a suggestion box. It is not a corporate wellness committee. It is the replacement of the downward ownership relation with a horizontal one, scaled to a shop, a hospital, a grid, a warehouse, a school. Federation is how those councils coordinate without becoming a new head office. A federation is voluntary, recallable, and functional: it exists to settle what a single council cannot settle alone (a supply chain, a standard, a disaster, a transport corridor) and it dissolves back into its parts when the function is not needed. No new sovereign is created. No shareholder remnant is retained as a courtesy. The people who do the producing decide the producing.

This is not a command economy by another name, and it is not a market in the sense that Chapter 1 critiqued, the sense in which a favored company writes the rules of a game it also plays. Coordination is real. Complexity is real. Kaczynski was not wrong that complexity distributes competence. He was wrong, and I have said so, that the only exit is the one he chose. The syndicalist answer to complexity is not a return to a mythical cottage. It is the insistence that the people who already hold the distributed competence (the nurses, the line workers, the drivers, the teachers, the technicians) are the people who should federate it, rather than having it federated over their heads by a class that owns the federation as property.

Direct action, in the tradition this book argues from, does not mean a street fight. It means the people concerned acting on the situation that concerns them rather than petitioning a club to act on their behalf. A strike is direct action. A cooperative that takes a failing plant and runs it is direct action. A mutual-aid network that does the work a hollowed municipality has abandoned is direct action. A refusal to produce for a shipment the producers have had no say in is direct action. None of these require a vanguard, a personality cult, or a secret committee. All of them require the thing Chapter 3 and Chapter 4 are designed to prevent: a population that recognizes itself as capable of acting together without waiting for permission from the institutions that currently name permission.

I am not predicting that this will happen on a timetable, and I am not dressing it up as inevitable in the sense that would spare anyone the work. Inevitability is a cousin of the absolute cause Camus warned against. If the councils are treated as a future so bright that the present population can be treated as fuel, the alternative has already become the thing it opposed. The measure remains the human being in front of you. A federation that cannot tell a face from a five-year plan is only a new club.

What the alternative dissolves, if it dissolves anything, is corporate rule over daily life: the right of a shareholder remnant to set the pace of a warehouse, the content of a newsroom, the terms of a lease, the availability of a medicine, and, as Chapter 5 argued, the slope of a war. Dissolution is not confiscation-as-spectacle and it is not a night of broken glass. It is the transfer, by organized withdrawal of legitimacy and by the construction of replacement institutions, of decisions that are already being made by people who do not do the work to people who do. Peaceful, in the Author's Note, was not a decorative adjective. It was a constraint. A book that has spent a chapter with Camus cannot end by blessing a sacred violence in the name of the councils. The councils either remain a rebellion that says yes to a human limit, or they become an office of the machine with different letterhead.

## We The People, Without the Costume

The founding documents of the country this book is dated to (2026, the two-hundred-and-fiftieth year) contain a phrase that has been asked to do too much ceremonial work and not enough institutional work. We The People is printed on souvenirs. It is printed, also, at the top of a preamble that claimed a power of self-constitution. The chapters of this book have been an argument that the power was captured, early and then repeatedly, by a class that found it more convenient to keep the phrase than to keep the practice.

Putting the phrase back to work does not mean a new round of campaign merch. It means the people who already constitute the country in the only sense that matters (the people who feed it, nurse it, wire it, teach it, drive it, clean it, and bury it) constituting the decisions that currently sit in the club. That is a domestic program before it is a foreign one, because a public without a lever at home will not acquire one at the water's edge. Chapter 5's flashpoint will be replaced by another flashpoint for as long as the interior arrangement produces slopes and calls them destinies.

The peaceful path is slower to photograph than the violent one, which is why the feed will always prefer a story about a mob. The feed is not the audience of this book. The audience is the reader who has recognized, in the factory and the classroom and the file and the ballot and the slope, a single machine, and who is willing to do the unphotogenic work of building the institutions that could replace it. Unions that answer to their members. Cooperatives that answer to the people who work them. Federations that answer to the councils that compose them. Schools that teach the turning of analytical tools onto the assigning institution. A press that cannot be sorted, at least not cheaply, by the worthy/unworthy filter. A political life that does not begin and end in a duopoly's primary.

None of this requires a reader to become a saint, a martyr, or a character in someone else's manifesto. It requires a reader to stop treating corporate rule as weather. Weather is not dissolvable. Arrangements are. The East India Company was an arrangement. The panopticon is an arrangement. The duopoly is an arrangement. Primacy is an arrangement. Arrangements are maintained because they reward the behavior that sustains them and starve the behavior that would threaten them. The counter is not a secret. It is the construction of a reward structure in which the people who do the work are the people who set the terms, and in which a human limit, Camus's limit, is permitted to interrupt a cause that has begun to talk like a god.

Glubb will not be cheated by a slogan. Empires of the kind he counted did not last forever because they had not yet read a pamphlet. They lasted until the interior hollowing and the exterior overextension became the same fact. The United States in its two-hundred-and-fiftieth year is entitled to no exemption from that rhyme, and it is entitled, also, to no fatalism about it. Fatalism is the decadent form of insight. It is stage five without the trouble of stage six's repair. This book has tried to refuse both the exemption and the fatalism. The machine is real. The alternative is nameable. The transfer is peaceful or it is a contradiction of the measure the book has spent its length defending.

Camus wrote that a rebel is a person who says no and, in the same gesture, yes. The no, here, is to corporate rule, to the hybrid that socializes risk and privatizes profit, to the filter that sorts faces, to the slope that walks a public toward a wider war in its name. The yes is to the councils, to the federations, to the work already being done, and to the people doing it. We The People is the yes, if it is allowed to mean the people rather than the costume.

What that yes looks like on an ordinary Tuesday is deliberately unspectacular, which is why the feed will not film it. A hospital whose nurses and technicians set staffing rather than receiving it from a private-equity dashboard. A warehouse whose pace is a matter for the people walking it, not for an algorithm maximizing units against bathroom time. A newsroom that cannot be sorted, cheaply, by the worthy/unworthy filter because it is answerable to the people producing the reporting rather than to an advertising market. A grid, a rail line, a school: the same relation, federated where a single council cannot see the whole corridor. Direct action, again, is the people concerned acting on the situation that concerns them. It is a strike, a cooperative, a mutual-aid network, a refusal to treat a shipment the producers had no say in as weather. It is not a vanguard, not a personality, and not a sacred date.

I have not offered a foreign policy because a public without those Tuesday institutions will not be permitted a humane one. Chapter 5's flashpoint will be replaced by another flashpoint for as long as the interior arrangement produces slopes and calls them destinies. The people living in the blast radius, in the sanctions line, and in the safe room are not a subplot of an American self-help project. They are the measure. Camus's limit applies to them as it applies to the warehouse floor. A council that cannot hear them is a club. A manifesto that forgets them in order to complete a domestic argument has walked, in prose, the same slope it spent a chapter naming.

The ruins will wait. They always have. They are patient in a way institutions of extraction are not, because the ruins do not need a quarterly return. What a living population needs is not a better speech about eternity. It is a set of institutions that can still tell a face from a jersey when the speech is over. That is the whole book. The rest was evidence.
