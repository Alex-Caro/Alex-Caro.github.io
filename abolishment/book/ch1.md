# Chapter 1: 250-Year Empire Theory

## A Pattern That Does Not Care About Exceptionalism

I would like to begin Chapter 1 with a brief introduction to the hypothesis of the 250-Year Empire Theory credited to historian Sir John Glubb, in which he disturbingly found that the largest historical empires all tended to follow similar patterns. These patterns can be of smaller and larger size but can be broken down into six stages. Late in life Glubb sat down with a stack of imperial chronologies and noticed something a patriotic education is designed not to notice: Assyria, Persia, the Greek world after Alexander, the Roman Republic, the Roman Empire, the Arab caliphates, the Mamluks, the Ottomans, Spain, Romanov Russia, and Britain all clustered, in his accounting, around two and a half centuries of real supremacy. Some ran a little short. Some ran a little long. The average was close enough to 250 years that a serious person has to decide whether to treat the number as a curiosity or as a warning.

I am writing this in June 2026, one month before the United States marks 250 years from the Declaration of Independence. That is not a cute coincidence I invented for a title. It is the calendar doing Glubb's arithmetic out loud. The civic religion of this country has always insisted that the United States is the exception to every rule that bound every other empire: we are a republic, not an empire; a city on a hill, not a garrison; a free market, not a court of favorites. Glubb's point, and the point of this chapter, is that the self-story an empire tells about its uniqueness is one of the most reliable symptoms of the stage it is in, not evidence that it has escaped the pattern. Spain believed it had been chosen. Britain believed it had been chosen. Rome believed it had been chosen. Chosen-ness is not a survival strategy. It is a lullaby.

> The Earth is littered with the ruins of empires that once believed they were eternal.

Camille Paglia's line sits at the front of this book because it is the one sentence that Glubb's tables, Chomsky's ledgers, and a walk through any former imperial capital all agree on. The ruins are not only marble. They are also the habits of a people who cannot imagine the arrangement ending, and so cannot imagine reforming it until the ending has already begun. I want to be precise about what I am and am not claiming. I am not a numerologist. I do not think a nation dies on the morning of its 250th birthday because a British general once averaged some dates. I think a nation that has spent 250 years converting land, labor, and law into concentrated private power, and that now socializes the losses of that power while privatizing its gains, is exhibiting every trait Glubb filed under decline, and that calling those traits "the free market" does not make them younger.

Glubb's stages are worth naming in full before this chapter spends any more time on the American case, because each later chapter of this book effectively expands on one of them. Stage 1 is the Age of Pioneers: a hard, relatively poor, relatively cohesive group breaks a new political fact into the world, usually by walking away from an older extraction regime they have decided they will no longer fund. Stage 2 is the Age of Conquests: the new fact becomes a territorial fact, and the energy of founding turns outward. Stage 3 is the Age of Commerce: territory is converted into trade routes, workshops, banks, and the first truly large private fortunes. Stage 4 is the Age of Affluence: the wealth of commerce becomes so abundant that a generation inherits what a prior generation built, and the old link between effort and reward begins to fray. Stage 5 is the Age of Intellect: a nation rich enough to stop working with its hands turns to argument, philosophy, criticism, and increasingly abstract self-examination, and Glubb warned that this stage produces brilliant diagnosis and very little unified action. Stage 6 is the Age of Decadence: wealth, comfort, and internal argument curdle into a defensiveness that mistakes decline for stability, right up until the moment it is neither.

Late State Capitalism is this book's name for what happens when the Age of Affluence refuses to end on schedule. Glubb was describing agrarian and mercantile empires. We are living inside a financialized one. The mapping is not poetic. It is mechanical. The rest of this chapter is the mechanism.

## The Age of Pioneers and the Taxes That Founded a Nation

The Age of Pioneers, in the American telling, is a costume drama: pious farmers, a harbor, a signature, a bell. The actual founding was a tax revolt conducted by men who understood extraction from the receiving end and then, with a speed that still takes the breath, built a new extraction machine of their own. The colonists fled over a handful of taxes they had grown tired of paying. That sentence is taught to children. What is not taught with the same patience is which taxes, who they were designed to serve, and how closely their structure resembles the corporate-state arrangements this book is about.

It is worth slowing down on the years after the Seven Years' War, because that is when the British imperial system stopped being a distant rumor and became a set of statutes with customs officers attached. Britain had just finished a global war against France and was holding a mountain of debt. The North American colonies had been, from London's point of view, beneficiaries of that war: French power was broken on the continent, and the settlers' western claims looked safer. Parliament's conclusion was the conclusion every overstretched empire reaches. Someone was going to pay. The colonists, who had no seat at Westminster, were an attractive someone. Each of the four acts that followed shared a structure that will recur throughout this book: a distant authority extracting value from a population that had no seat at the table where the extraction was decided.

The Sugar Act of 1764, formally the American Revenue Act, is usually remembered as a tax on sweetness. It was that, and it was also something more modern. It lowered the old molasses duty on paper, from sixpence to threepence a gallon, and then did the thing the old Molasses Act of 1733 had never seriously done: it tried to collect. It expanded the jurisdiction of admiralty courts, which meant a merchant could be tried without a jury. It was not only a price. It was a compliance regime, an early ancestor of the apparatus in Chapter 3. Molasses fed rum. Rum fed wages, trade, and the triangular traffic in enslaved people that the same founding generation would later decline to name honestly. When you tax the everyday, you are inserting the state into the metabolism of ordinary life.

I will come back to sugar, because sugar never left. It changed costume. The Stamp Act of 1765 did the same insertion by another door: the first serious direct internal tax, reaching newspapers, legal documents, licenses, almanacs, and even playing cards. A tax on the deck is a tax that has stopped pretending to be about war debt. Parliament repealed it in 1766 and, in the same season, passed the Declaratory Act asserting the right to bind the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." The tax left. The claim stayed. The Townshend Acts of 1767 widened the net to glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea, paired with new customs enforcement in Boston, and tried a lobbyist's distinction between internal and external duties. The colonists looked at the structure. A distant legislature was still writing prices onto daily goods for people who had not voted for the rate. I will not pretend the colonial elite were saints of democracy. Many of them were slaveowners. Many wanted a freer hand in western land. Hypocrisy about equality does not cancel the accuracy of their complaint about extraction without a seat at the table. It only tells you that the men who made the complaint would, within a generation, fail to apply it to people they themselves extracted from. That failure set the tone for the circus that followed over the next twenty-five decades.

## Sugar Then, Syrup Now

The Sugar Act taxed an everyday product, foreign sugars and molasses, used in numerous beverages at the time. It is almost comical to think how the same tax would play out if it were suddenly introduced tomorrow. I challenge any reader to go a mere 72 hours without some form of sugar, then report back and let me know how the masses feel. This is not a wellness dare. It is a political instrument. Sugar in the eighteenth century was already a world-system commodity: Caribbean islands worked by enslaved Africans, New England distilleries, British counting houses, African coasts, and a European sweet tooth that had been trained in a few generations to treat a rare spice as a necessity. When Parliament put a duty on molasses, it was not regulating a treat. It was putting a meter on a circuit that already ran through bodies, ships, and banks.

The modern American version of that circuit does not look like a hogshead of molasses on a Boston wharf. It looks like a two-liter bottle. The sugar we meet now takes form in our "soft" drinks, or rather in high-fructose corn syrup that is heavily restricted in countless countries around the world. It is in the desserts we eat after a long day of being worked to the bone. It is in the short bursts of energy countless Americans rely on merely to get by and simply do it again the next day. It is without surprise that we are number one in average sugar intake at 126.4 grams per person, per day. Sit with that number against the World Health Organization's suggestion that free sugars stay nearer 25 grams, or at most 50, if a person wants to stay on the kinder side of the metabolic ledger. We are not nibbling past a guideline. We are swimming in a subsidy.

High-fructose corn syrup is not a law of nature. It is an industrial outcome. American corn policy, especially from the 1970s onward, produced mountains of cheap corn. Processors learned to turn that corn into a sweetener that could replace cane and beet sugar in almost anything that came in a can, a bottle, or a wrapper. In the early 1980s the major soda companies switched. The switch is sometimes narrated as a mere technical substitution, one molecule for another, a triumph of food science. It was a political substitution. Cane sugar had foreign producers and a quota system. Corn had Iowa, Illinois, a handful of firms with names that never appear on the bottle, and a farm bill that socialized the cost of overproduction while the sweetener's profits remained private. If you want a compact illustration of late state capitalism before this chapter has even finished defining the term, you could do worse than this: the public pays to grow more corn than a free market would justify, a processor turns the surplus into a sweetener, a beverage company sells the sweetener back to the public as comfort after a double shift, and the metabolic bill arrives decades later in the form of diabetes, dental collapse, and a health system that will be described, when the invoices come, as an individual responsibility problem.

I am not a nutritionist, and this book is not a diet. In 1764 the staple was molasses, the distant authority was Parliament, and the colonists at least knew they were being taxed. In 2026 the staple is a corn derivative, the distant authority is a thicket of agribusiness and captured committees, and the language of the transaction is "choice." You chose the soda that gets you through the last three hours of a shift designed to require it. Choice is a real word. It is also a curtain. Glubb's pioneers revolted over duties they could see printed in a statute. Late state capitalism prefers duties they can taste and still be told are not political. The colonial molasses trade was braided with slavery. The modern syrup trade is braided with a labor market that needs cheap calories to keep cheap hours possible. Peter Joseph's language of structural violence, which I will use more heavily in Chapter 2, fits here without much translation. No one is standing over the average American with a musket. The violence is in the arrangement that makes the soda the rational short-term solution to a long-term exhaustion the same arrangement produced.

## The Stamp, the Townshend Duties, and the Everyday Made Fiscal

The Stamp Act's genius, from London's point of view, was coverage. A duty on molasses hits drinkers and distillers. A stamp on legal paper hits anyone who marries, sues, dies, advertises, or reads. Printers became militants almost overnight, not because printers are naturally revolutionary, but because their inventory had been turned into a tax farm. When a government taxes the deck of cards on the table, it has announced that there is no corner of ordinary time too small to meter. Colonial resistance was, among other things, a rehearsal for a kind of political literacy this book is trying to recover: the ability to see a technical instrument as a relation of power. Underneath the constitutional pamphlets is a simpler sentence. You do not get to price our days from an ocean away.

The Townshend program tried to answer that sentence with a distinction worthy of a modern lobbyist. We will not tax you inside your colony. We will tax the goods as they enter, and we will pay the salaries of governors and judges from the take, so that those men will be less dependent on your local assemblies. The duties were not only revenue. They were an attempt to uncouple imperial officials from local consent. I cannot think of a cleaner eighteenth-century preview of the modern revolving door, in which the people who write the rules are paid from a stream that does not run through the public that has to live under the rules. A system that has decided extraction is legitimate will always describe evasion as the crisis, and enforcement as the remedy, and will never quite get around to asking whether the extraction had a right to exist. By 1770 most of the Townshend duties were repealed. The tea duty was kept, on purpose, as a marker of Parliament's right. That leftover duty is the hinge into the Tea Act.

## The Tea Act Was a Bailout

The Tea Act of 1773, remembered today mostly for the harbor party it provoked, was in fact a bailout dressed as a tax cut, structured to prop up the failing East India Company by granting it a monopoly on colonial tea sales. I want that sentence to do a lot of work, because if a reader takes only one structural analogy from the Age of Pioneers into the present, it should be this one.

The East India Company was not a charming firm with a logo and a catalog. It was a chartered corporation with shareholders in London, a private army in the subcontinent, the power to make war and collect taxes in Bengal, and a balance sheet that had become a problem for the British state. By the early 1770s it was sitting on enormous inventories of unsold tea, rattling from the credit crisis of 1772, and looking at a Parliament that had already been forced, in the Regulating Act of 1773, to treat the Company's governance as a public emergency. The Tea Act was the commercial half of that emergency management. It let the Company sell tea directly into the American colonies, cutting out colonial merchants, and it kept the remaining Townshend duty on tea so that the constitutional claim would not die. The tea would arrive cheaper than smuggled Dutch tea. Consumers, on a naive reading, should have been pleased. A good was going to be less expensive.

The colonists who dumped that tea into Boston Harbor on December 16, 1773, were not rejecting cheap tea. They were rejecting a system that would let a favored corporation write the rules of a market it also competed in, a complaint that will sound uncomfortably familiar to anyone who has watched a bailout unfold in this century. Cheap was the bait. The hook was monopoly plus a leftover duty whose whole purpose was to make the colonists swallow Parliament's supremacy with their caffeine. Local merchants who had been the middle of the trade would be pushed aside. A corporation that had already looted Bengal, and whose collapse threatened London credit, would be offered the colonies as a captive outlet. The public would get a discount. The Company would get survival. The principle of consent would get a demonstration that it was negotiable.

That is the structure of a bailout. A firm becomes too entangled with the state's finances, or too large in a staple trade, or too connected to the men who write statutes, and the solution offered to the public is always a version of cheaper tea: a lower sticker price, a preserved brand, a promise that ordinary people will not feel the rescue. The thing that is actually being rescued is the arrangement. Dumping the tea was vandalism. It was also literacy. I do not romanticize the Sons of Liberty. I am interested in what they were able to read: a corporate rescue mission disguised as consumer relief.

The British response, the so-called Coercive or Intolerable Acts of 1774, closed Boston's port, altered Massachusetts government, and made the empire's bet explicit: the rescue of the Company and the humiliation of the port would be pursued together. That is how a tax dispute becomes a war. Not because tea is mystical, but because a state that has fused its prestige to a corporation's balance sheet cannot permit a public to refuse the fusion. I invite the reader to hold that sentence until we reach 2008, and then to decide how antique the eighteenth century really is. The Declaration of 1776 comes out of that sequence: magnificent and compromised, a political language several sizes larger than the political economy it was willing to confront. Slaveowners who wanted to be free set the tone. Glubb's first stage is not a moral halo. A hard group of people created a new polity because an old extraction regime had become intolerable to them, then built a successor regime that would spend two centuries arguing about how widely the word "them" was allowed to stretch.

## The Age of Conquests

Glubb's second stage follows the founding generation as the young nation extends its reach outward. In our case that means westward expansion and the acquisitions that turned thirteen colonies into a continental power, and then into something that could not keep its hands off the Caribbean and the Pacific. American schoolbooks prefer the word "settlement." Settlement is what you call conquest when the people being conquered are going to be written out of the subsequent chapters.

The Northwest Ordinance of 1787, the Louisiana Purchase of 1803, the seizure and purchase of Florida, the annexation of Texas, the Oregon compromise, the Mexican-American War of 1846-1848, the Gadsden Purchase, Alaska, Hawaii, the Spanish-American War of 1898, the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam, the manipulations around Panama: this is not a list I offer as a civics recitation. It is the map of a country that could not sit still inside its own speeches about consent. Louisiana doubled the land at a stroke because Napoleon needed money and Jefferson needed the Mississippi. The legality of buying an empire from a man who had taken it from someone else was always a little mystical, and the people who lived in the purchased landscape were not at the table. They rarely are. The Mexican War, which even a young Congressman named Lincoln had the sense to distrust, added a northern half of Mexico to the United States and added a new shelf of arguments about the expansion of slavery. Turner's frontier thesis would later treat the moving edge as the factory of American character. Glubb would have recognized it as Stage 2 doing what Stage 2 does: converting founding energy into land, and land into the precondition of commerce.

I want to stay a moment with the people the frontier thesis treats as scenery. Indian Removal, the Trail of Tears, a century of broken treaties, the reservation system, Wounded Knee: these are not sidebars to the Age of Conquests. They are the method. A republic that had just finished complaining about distant authority proceeded to make itself a distant authority over nations that had not invited it. The Supreme Court could say, as it did in Worcester v. Georgia, that a state could not simply dissolve a tribal nation, and Andrew Jackson could proceed as if the Court had cleared its throat and nothing more. That gap between parchment and practice is not a glitch in the Age of Conquests. It is the Age of Conquests. Law is what the expanding power is willing to have bound itself with today. Tomorrow's land hunger will write a new memo.

The overseas chapter of American conquest is usually segregated into a different course, as if 1898 were a mood swing rather than a continuation. It was a continuation. By the time the United States took the Philippines from Spain, and then fought a brutal counterinsurgency against Filipinos who had not volunteered to be purchased in the deal, the pioneer republic had become an overseas empire with the usual equipment: racial justifications, naval bases, and a business class that could see markets in the smoke. The reflex does not retire when the continental frontier closes. It looks for a map with more water on it.

Conquest feeds commerce. That is the whole link between Stage 2 and Stage 3. Land taken from indigenous nations became farms, then railroads, then mineral claims. Harbors taken from Spain became coaling stations, then the logistics of a corporate state that would eventually need the whole planet as a warehouse and a customer. A reader who wants to believe that the United States is not an empire has to explain this sequence without the word. I have never seen it done honestly. We did the second stage. We did it thoroughly. We then told ourselves a story in which the doing was accidental, defensive, or a gift. Every empire tells that story.

## The Age of Commerce

The Age of Commerce follows conquest, as the nation's energy shifts from acquiring territory to building the industrial and financial systems that convert territory into wealth. This is the subject of Chapter 2 in its inner workings, the factory whistle and the re-engineering of the human day, but it has to be named here as a stage or Glubb's sequence falls apart in the American case.

Commerce, in the innocent textbook, means steamboats, cotton, clipper ships, the telegraph, the transcontinental railroad, and a million people getting slightly less poor. Some of that happened. What also happened is that the corporation, which had been a rare legal tool for bridges and turnpikes, became the default container for private power, and then became a person for the purposes of the Fourteenth Amendment in the long shadow of Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad in 1886. A civil war amendment written in the blood of emancipation was, within a generation, a shelter for railroad capital. I do not need to believe in a cartoon of judges cackling in a back room to see the outcome. A legal system built by and for men of property will, when invited to interpret the word "person," notice the railroad. The Age of Commerce is the age in which that noticing becomes common sense.

The railroad is the American East India Company in slower motion: chartered, subsidized, armed with land grants that dwarfed the Homestead acreage actually available to ordinary families, capable of buying legislatures, capable of setting the prices that decided whether a farmer ate. The credit mobilization, the panics of 1873 and 1893, the House of Morgan, Standard Oil's command of a staple, Carnegie's command of steel, the Pinkertons as a private army for a private government: this is not the free market of the pamphlet. It is a court of favorites with better machines. When the Sherman Act arrived in 1890 it arrived as a statue in the window. Sometimes it was even used. More often it was used on unions than on the combinations it had been sold as a weapon against. A law that can be turned against the people it was advertised to protect is not a failure of drafting only. It is a demonstration of who holds the handle.

By the early twentieth century the Age of Commerce had grown a central bank, a federal income tax, and a professional managerial class that could speak the language of public interest while routing the benefits toward balance sheets. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 is many things, some of them genuinely responsive to a real history of panics. It is also the moment at which money itself becomes an administrative object at national scale, which is to say the moment at which a later generation will be able to treat the stock market's health as a proxy for the nation's health and have the institutional equipment to act on that confusion. Stage 3 builds the plumbing Stage 4 and Stage 5 will live inside, and the plumbing was never politically neutral.

What Glubb saw in other empires as merchants succeeding warriors, we can see as finance succeeding the frontier. The warrior does not vanish. He gets a pension and a contractor. The merchant does not remain a merchant. He becomes a shareholder, then a fund, then a claim on future labor that can be traded without meeting the laborer. Chapter 2 will stay with the laborer. This chapter only needs to insist that the Age of Commerce is not a story about goods. It is a story about the legal and financial instruments that let goods, land, and time be owned at a distance, which is the precondition for everything Chomsky will name as profit over people.

## The Age of Affluence

The Age of Affluence follows commerce, and this is the stage most Americans alive today have only ever known, a period in which the wealth generated by earlier stages becomes so abundant that the connection between effort and reward starts to fray for the generation inheriting it rather than building it. Glubb watched this happen in empires that measured wealth in land and bullion. We measure it in asset prices, which makes the fraying easier to miss because the number on the screen can rise while the floor under a life is thinning.

The mid-twentieth-century United States is the showcase. Mass production, a world war that left other industrial centers in rubble, a labor movement that had clawed out a share, a GI Bill that was real for many white veterans and a closed door for many Black ones, suburbs, highways, a kitchen full of machines: this is the photograph the country still tries to crawl back into. I do not deny the photograph. A union machinist in 1965 could often buy a house, keep a spouse out of the wage, and expect a pension that was not a rumor. That was not nothing. It was also not a natural fact of capitalism. It was a historically specific bargain, struck in the shadow of depression, war, and a rival superpower that made American elites temporarily interested in the loyalty of their own working class. Bargains have terms. They expire when the people who signed them die, or when the threat that motivated them recedes, or when a new financial technology makes it possible to keep the profits without keeping the peace.

The fraying has a date range even if it does not have a single morning. Productivity and typical wages, which had traveled together for a generation, begin to separate in the 1970s. The oil shocks, the end of Bretton Woods, stagflation, and the Volcker shock are the weather. The union-busting, the capital mobility, the rewriting of tax and labor law, the rise of the shareholder-value gospel associated with the 1980s, are the climate. Affluence remains the official weather report. Houses get larger. Consumer goods get cheaper in nominal terms. Credit gets easier. What quietly changes is the ownership of the future. A generation inherits the stuff and loses the claim. Pensions become 401(k)s, which is to say they become exposure to the same asset markets that the Age of Commerce built for other people. Housing becomes an asset class before it is a shelter. Education becomes a debt instrument before it is a public good. The children of affluence are told they are rich because the country's aggregate numbers are large, and they are told they are failures if those numbers do not appear in their own accounts.

Glubb thought affluence produced softness. I think it produces a more specific American injury: a population trained to measure itself against a postwar bargain that the owners of the country have quietly withdrawn from, while the advertising of the bargain continues at full volume. The result is not simply envy. It is a politics of bewilderment. People work. The old story says work is the ticket. The ticket does not punch. Someone has to be blamed. Late state capitalism is very generous with suggestions about whom to blame, and very shy about suggesting the shareholder. Affluence that cannot say who captures the surplus, and who eats the risk when the floor gives, is not a resting place. It is a waiting room.

## The Age of Intellect

The Age of Intellect follows affluence, in which a nation wealthy enough to stop working with its hands turns instead to argument, philosophy, and increasingly abstract forms of self-examination, a stage Glubb warned tends to produce brilliant critique and very little unified action. If you want a one-sentence review of American public life in the early twenty-first century, you could do worse than that.

We are drowning in diagnosis. Journalism, when it is still funded, can be superb. Academic work on inequality, climate, corporate concentration, and labor is voluminous. Podcasts will talk to you for three hours about the Federal Reserve. Social media will serve you a new theory of decline before breakfast. This book is, I am aware, a citizen of the same stage. I am sitting with Chomsky and Glubb and Camus and Peter Joseph, and with a mathematical diagnosis I will take from a man whose methods I reject, and I am adding another stack of pages to a country that is already unusually good at describing its own injuries. Glubb's warning is that the stack is not the same thing as a repair. A society can become fluent in the language of its own unraveling and still be unable to stop pulling the thread.

The American university system is the cathedral of this stage: beautiful, expensive, and increasingly far from the original faith. More people know more true things about power than at any time in the country's history. Fewer of those things seem able to travel from a seminar to a statute without being translated into the dialect of donors. Chapter 3 will take up the classroom. Here I only need the Glubb observation: intellect becomes a mass occupation once affluence has produced a class that does not have to grow food. The pioneers had a harbor to close. We have a literature review.

I do not sneer at intellect. This book would be a tantrum without it. I sneer at the alibi intellect has become. Brilliant critique is how a Stage 5 empire demonstrates that it is still awake. Unified action is how a people demonstrate that they are still a people. The gap between the two is where decadence breeds, because a public that has been trained to treat argument as completion will experience any demand for organization as a lowering of the tone. Anarcho-syndicalism, the tradition I named in the introduction, is in this sense an insult to Stage 5 manners. It does not want a better panel. It wants production organized by the people doing the producing, federated upward through voluntary unions rather than owned downward through shareholders. That sentence is not a metaphor and it is not a seminar. It is a description of a different handle on the same machine.

## The Age of Decadence

Finally comes the Age of Decadence, in which the accumulated wealth, comfort, and internal argument curdle into a defensiveness that mistakes decline for stability, right up until the moment it is neither. Glubb's list of symptoms, drawn from older empires, is uncomfortably easy to translate. Frivolity as mass culture. Celebrity as a substitute for character. A widening gap between the military's rank and file and the classes that decide on wars. A taste for luxury that outruns the productive base. A public discourse that treats every sign of strain as either a conspiracy or a reason to double down on the official story. An anxious hunt for internal enemies. The conviction, immune to evidence, that this time the gods have made an exception.

I want to handle one of Glubb's observations with care, because in his pages it can be read as a complaint about foreigners, and this book is not in the business of laundering nativism. Empires in their late stages often experience migration and then blame the migrants for the late stage. The causation is usually backward. People move toward money and away from the wreckage of earlier imperial adventures; the wreckage and the money were both produced by the empire. Scapegoating the newcomer is how a Stage 6 public refuses to audit the owners. When I say decadence, I am not talking about the language spoken in a neighborhood. I am talking about a ruling arrangement that cannot imagine sharing power, cannot imagine letting a firm fail, cannot imagine a foreign policy of restraint, and cannot imagine a culture whose heroes are not, in one way or another, rich.

Defensiveness is the emotional weather of this stage. Criticism of the corporate state is recoded as hatred of the country. Criticism of a war is recoded as hatred of the people asked to fight it. Criticism of a bailout is recoded as naivete about "the economy," as if the economy were a weather system and not a set of statutes. The official story has a genius for stealing the flag from the people who have to live under the statutes. I do not cede the flag. The pioneers, at their best, were people who refused to fund an arrangement they had not consented to. That refusal is more patriotic, if the word still means anything, than a stadium full of flyovers paid for by the same firms that will be first in line when the next rescue is drafted.

Decadence, as I am using it, is not dancing and it is not sex and it is not the fact that young people like different music. Those are the complaints of men who have confused their youth with a moral order. Decadence is the inability of a rich society to use its richness for anything except the reproduction of the claims that produced it. Hospitals become revenue centers. Universities become brands. Housing becomes a speculative sport. Food becomes a delivery app taking a cut of a cook. Politics becomes a subscription. Stage 6 is not a movie about villains. It is a movie about a machine that can metabolize virtue and still output the same ownership. Camus would say that the absurd appears when a hunger for meaning collides with a world that answers in slogans. The danger Glubb recorded is that a people can live inside that emptiness for a long time, calling it stability, because the lights are still on. The lights are not the empire. The ownership of the switch is the empire. Late state capitalism is the name of the switch.

## Late State Capitalism

Late State Capitalism is this book's name for what happens when the Age of Affluence refuses to end on schedule. Glubb was describing agrarian and mercantile empires, but the pattern maps onto a financialized economy with unsettling precision. Affluence is supposed, in the old sequence, to pass into intellect and then into decadence as the productive and martial virtues thin out. In the American case the sequence is scrambled by a trick older empires could not quite perform: the state is enrolled as the insurer of private claims, so that affluence can be extended for the balance-sheet class even as it is withdrawn from everyone else. The photograph of 1965 stays on the wall. The pension does not stay in the contract. When the gap becomes politically dangerous, the solution is not to restore the bargain. The solution is to socialize whatever losses might threaten the photograph's owners.

That arrangement needs a name that is more specific than "capitalism," because capitalism in the textbook sense includes a discipline this arrangement has discarded. In the textbook, firms compete, prices signal, failures fail, and the state is a night watchman with a limited kit. I am not nostalgic for the textbook. The textbook never described the East India Company, or the railroads, or a sugar quota, or a Federal Reserve backstop. But the textbook at least had a story about consequence. Late state capitalism keeps the vocabulary of the story and quietly removes the consequence for a particular class of actor. Profit remains private. Risk becomes a public utility. The rest of us are invited to call this the market, and to feel uneducated if we notice the invitation is a command.

The "state" in the term is not a slur against public hospitals or fire departments. I am not an apostle of the night watchman. Anarcho-syndicalism wants public life; it just does not want public life run as a holding company for private capital. The state I am naming is the one that writes tax codes with doors in them, staffs regulatory agencies from the industries they supervise, treats a stock index as a vital organ, and can find trillions when a bank is on fire and becomes suddenly meticulous when a renter is on fire. That state is not the opposite of the corporation. It is the corporation's emergency department, legal department, and occasionally its ministry of propaganda.

I said earlier that late state capitalism is affluence refusing to end on schedule. The refusal is not mysterious. The people who inherited the claims of Stages 2 and 3 have no incentive to let a cycle complete if the completion would mean their claims reprice. So they use the one institution with a printing press, a court system, and an army to pin the cycle in place. That is not a conspiracy in the cinematic sense. It is an alignment of incentives so stable it does not need to be coordinated in a smoke-filled room. A thousand separate boards, facing a thousand separate quarters, discover the same prayer: if this fails, someone with a flag must catch it. Over time the prayer becomes an assumption. Over a little more time the assumption becomes a rule. Too big to fail is the rule's slogan. The slogan is dishonest twice. The institution is too connected to favored political circuits to fail, and the failure is not prevented, it is moved, into households that were never too big to evict.

## Profit Over People

Noam Chomsky, in *Profit Over People*, argued that the last several decades of American policy have been organized around a version of capitalism in which risk is socialized and profit is privatized: the public absorbs the losses when a bank or an airline or an auto manufacturer fails, while the gains during the good years accrue almost entirely to shareholders and executives. I want to stay with that book long enough to let it do more than furnish a slogan, because Chomsky is not, in that text, performing a mood. He is describing a program.

The program has a respectable name, neoliberalism, and a respectable set of pamphlets about efficiency, comparative advantage, and getting the government out of the way. Chomsky's useful rudeness is to look at the pamphlets and then look at the statutes. The government is never out of the way of a defense contractor, a pharmaceutical patent, a bank with a liquidity problem, or a grain trader living on a farm bill. The government is out of the way of a labor standard, a tenant, a debtor, a small competitor, a foreign population whose social provisions are described as distortions. "Free market" is a beam of light that falls on the poor. The rich stand in the shade of the state and call the shade nature. Chomsky's line, which I will paraphrase rather than tattoo on the page, is that this is not a failure of the ideology to live up to itself. It is the ideology. Neoliberal principles, in his account, are a project of insulating private power from public influence, of moving decisions from arenas where people can vote to arenas where people can only buy, and of calling the result freedom.

*Profit Over People* is also a book about democracy as a problem for this project. A population with actual civic power will, given time, demand that the gains of a society be less insanely concentrated, that the risks of a society be less insanely dumped on the people least able to carry them, that a doctor not be a luxury good, that a job not be a lottery. The neoliberal answer is not always a club. Often it is a story: the market has decided, the numbers will not permit, the alternative is Venezuela, the alternative is the 1970s, the alternative is your own laziness. Manufacturing consent, the older Chomsky and Herman title, is the communications wing of the same operation. Profit over people is the balance-sheet wing. Together they produce a public that can watch a rescue of its creditors and feel that the rescue was the adult thing to do.

I draw on Chomsky here not because I want the reader to salute a master, but because he named the inversion with a patience that American television still treats as bad manners. A student who cannot pay a medical bill is a moral problem. A bank that cannot pay its counterparties is a systemic event. The student will be given a lecture about personal responsibility. The bank will be given a facility. The pattern does not require a secret. It requires a class interest with the power to write the definition of "systemic." Organized things can be disorganized. A correct fear of chaos, the reversal-cost problem I will take up in Chapter 2, is not an argument for eternal blackmail. It is an argument for building a successor arrangement that can feed people without feeding the blackmail.

## Socialized Risk, from the East India Company to 2008

The Tea Act, as I argued, was a bailout dressed as a tax cut. Once you have seen the costume, you start seeing it in every century that follows. Nineteenth-century railroads lived on land grants and periodic rescues. Chrysler in 1979, the savings and loan disaster of the 1980s, airlines after 2001: each can be argued on particular merits. Particular merits are how a pattern hides. When a certain class of institution fails, the failure is redefined as a public problem. When a certain class of household fails, the failure is defined as a private lesson.

2008 is the classroom. I will not retell every CDO. A housing market had been turned into a feedlot for securities. Originators made loans designed to be sold, not held. Rating agencies sold AAA to products that were not AAA. Banks leveraged the result until a decline in house prices could knock a hole in the world. When the hole opened, the people in the houses were offered foreclosure, shame, and modification programs that often functioned as delay. The people with the securities were offered TARP, a Federal Reserve balance sheet that became a new continent, and a return to bonuses with a speed that still surprises. AIG was not too important to the idea of insurance to fail. It was too entangled with counterparties who had the power to define entanglement as the public interest. Lehman was allowed to die, which is sometimes cited as proof that the era still had discipline. Lehman is the exception that advertises the rule. If your death would price the claims of the connected, you will not be allowed to die in a way that prices those claims.

Homeowners, who were also "the system" in any honest accounting, were allowed to die in the civic sense by the millions. That is socialized risk with a surgical instrument. The risk that mattered to capital was moved onto the Treasury and the Fed. The risk that mattered to households was left to households. Chomsky's formula does not need a footnote here. It needs a look at a foreclosure docket and a look at a bonus pool in the same year. One recapitalization was treated as oxygen. The other was treated as a sermon about not borrowing what you cannot repay, as if the borrowing had not been the product the banks were selling.

There is a case, and I do not dismiss it, that letting GM and Chrysler vanish in 2008-2009 would have taken a region down with them. Workers are real. What I refuse is the amnesia. The plant was systemic. The body was a market. Workers got two-tier wages and a lecture about competitiveness. Executives got to keep a relationship to the state that had just proved itself, again, as a department of last resort. COVID-era policy repeated the geometry: household support real enough, for a window, to prove that poverty is a policy; firm support larger, faster, and compatible with buybacks, while grocery clerks were called essential and paid as if they were optional.

High-fructose corn syrup belongs in this bailout genealogy even though no one calls a farm bill a rescue. It is a standing rescue of a production system that would not exist at its present scale or price without public machinery, feeding a food system that then sells the surplus back to a tired public as comfort. The East India Company wanted the colonies as an outlet for tea it could not unload. Corn processors want the American bloodstream as an outlet for starch they cannot stop producing. The continuity is almost insultingly clear. A staple, a favored set of firms, a public told it is being served because the retail price is low, a bill that arrives later in a form that will not be itemized as a tax.

## When the State Becomes a Department of the Firm

Theodore Kaczynski's critique of industrial-technological society, discussed at length in Chapter 2, applies here too, because late state capitalism is what industrial complexity looks like once it has fully absorbed the state as one of its own subsystems. I will say this next sentence as plainly as I can, because I will not have this book misread on the point: I reject his violence without remainder. I will not reproduce his manifesto, I will not treat his crimes as a method, and I will not so much as gesture at a how-to. A man who mailed bombs forfeited any right to be followed. What I will take, in Chapter 2, is a diagnosis that can be stated without his name attached to a tactic: as a system's technological and administrative complexity rises, the practical autonomy of the people living inside it tends to fall. In the notation I will use there, dA/dC is negative. You do not need his life to see the derivative in this chapter's materials.

Regulatory agencies staffed by former industry executives, legislation drafted by the industries it regulates, and a monetary policy apparatus that treats the stock market's health as a proxy for the nation's health, all of it points toward a system optimizing for its own continuation rather than for the people living inside it. The revolving door is not a metaphor. It is a career. A person goes from a bank to a Treasury post to a bank. A person goes from a carrier to a communications commission to a carrier. A person goes from a viscera of the Pentagon's contractor world into the acquisition office that writes the contractor's future and then back again. Capture is too passive a word if it suggests a regulator who was standing still when the industry arrived. In many cases the industry sent the regulator, and then received the regulator home.

Once the state is a subsystem, certain reforms become almost grammatically impossible. You can get a new ethics pamphlet. You cannot easily get a world in which the failure of a particular firm is allowed to mean the failure of a particular firm, because the firm's nervous system is now braided with payrolls, pensions, supply chains, campaign accounts, and the Fed's idea of stability. That braiding is the reversal-cost problem in political costume. The people who own the wall will say, not always falsely, that removing it will crush the kitchen. The honest reply is not "then let the kitchen be crushed." The honest reply is that a wall allowed to become load-bearing by blackmail still has to be replaced, and that replacement is construction, not theater.

This is why I keep returning to anarcho-syndicalism rather than to a romance of collapse. If complexity has absorbed the state, then asking that same state to become, overnight, the hero of the story is a Stage 5 hobby. Workers' organizations that actually know how to run the kitchen are a different kind of answer. They do not require a bomb. They require the unfashionable work of federating people who already do the labor, until the claim "we cannot do this without that board of directors" becomes empirically laughable. I do not want a cabin as a national model. I want a world in which the electrical grid, the clinic, and the grain elevator exist and are not weapons. The dA/dC diagnosis says that weaponization is the default if complexity is owned as a commanding height. It does not say that the only alternative is to smash the height and live in the rubble. Abolishment, as this book uses the word, is the dissolution of corporate rule, a peaceful means to put power back into the hands of we the people. I will not let a murderer hijack that sentence.

## Two Hundred and Fifty Years Is Not a Metaphor

Glubb's table is easy to mock if you want to. Empires do not start on a birthday. Britain's "1700 to 1950" is a pedagogue's rounding. Rome is several stories glued together. The Arab world's political history does not fit a single bell curve. Fine. Mock the rounding. Then explain why the clustered duration keeps showing up, and why the inner sequence of pioneer energy, conquest, commerce, affluence, intellect, and decadent defensiveness keeps showing up even when the dates are fuzzy. I am less attached to 250 as a magic integer than I am to the sequence, and to the brute fact that the United States is now old in empire years while remaining, in its official mind, a forever-young exception.

Map the sequence onto the American calendar without pretending the borders are customs checkpoints. Pioneers: the long founding in the last third of the eighteenth century. Conquests: the nineteenth century's land hunger, with 1898 as an overseas encore. Commerce: the industrial and financial buildout from the Civil War through the early twentieth century. Affluence: the postwar decades that still govern our idea of a normal life. Intellect: the explosion of universities, media, and professional criticism from the 1960s onward. Decadence: not a year, a weather. Late state capitalism is the economic operating system of that weather, installed in the 1970s, upgraded in the 1980s, stress-tested in 2008, patched in 2020, still running in 2026. Britain's imperial century is the comparison Americans are least willing to sit with, because we were trained to think we left the family. We inherited a temptation: to treat the parent's decline as proof of the child's exemption. Glubb, who served the parent, did not believe in exemptions. Neither do the ruins Paglia mentioned.

July 2026 will be a pageant. There will be flags. There will be a story about 1776 that still cannot decide whether to include the slave, the woman, the indigenous nation, the indentured servant, or the merchant who wanted cheaper tea and a freer hand in western land. I am not asking anyone to spit on a picnic. I am asking that the 250th year be used for the one thing a 250th year is good for in Glubb's terms: an audit. What stage are we in. Who owns the stage. What of the pioneer complaint about extraction without a seat at the table is still true, and who is playing Parliament now.

The honest audit is not flattering. A distant authority still prices daily life. The authority is no longer a king with a German accent. It is a thicket of firms, funds, and captured offices that can close a hospital, raise a rent, deny a claim, set a fare, or freeze a wage without anyone involved being reachable by a vote. Representation has been reduced, for most people, to a choice between two fundraising operations that agree on the sanctity of the thicket. Chapter 4 is about that reduction. The point for this chapter is that the pioneer complaint has not been answered. It has been domesticated. We celebrate the men who dumped the tea, and we drink the syrup, and we watch the Company get its outlet, and we call ourselves the heirs of the dumpers.

## What I Mean by Abolishment

I close this chapter by returning to the note that opens the book, because Chapter 1 is otherwise a long way to travel without restating the destination. The following is not a violent piece and not a call to action in the sense prosecutors like to imagine. It is a political critique. "Abolishment" means a dissolution of American corporate rule, a critique of the pyramid scheme and rat race disguised as free-market capitalism, a peaceful means to put power back into the hands of we the people. I am not playing with the word. I mean the East India arrangement should end: the favored company, the captured state, the public as outlet and as insurer, the worker as a cost. I do not mean a romance of rubble. I do not mean a hunt. I do not mean a method that treats bodies as levers.

Anarcho-syndicalism is the positive content of that negative. Production organized by the people doing the producing. Federation upward through voluntary unions rather than ownership downward through shareholders. The state apparatus that props up the modern corporation treated as no more legitimate, and no more permanent, than the corporation itself. If that sounds impossible to a Stage 5 ear, it is worth remembering that limited liability corporations of the present scale would have sounded impossible to a seventeenth-century ear, and then were constructed, statute by statute, because a class of people needed them. Things that are constructed can be unconstructed. The work is legal, cultural, and above all practical: people learning to run the necessary complexity together so that the blackmail of reversal cost loses its teeth.

The chapters that follow trace how the hollowing Glubb described, and the inversion Chomsky named, reach the classroom, the surveillance camera, the ballot box, and finally the battlefield. Immediately in front of us is the engine. Chapter 2 traces the industrial and proletarian machinery that fed the empire's growth: the whistle that replaced the sun, the class that owns nothing but time, the apps that turned a factory into a pocket, and the uncomfortable diagnosis that complexity buys convenience at the price of autonomy. I will take that diagnosis seriously. I will not take a murderer's counsel about what to do with it. The pioneers, at their best, dumped a corporation's cargo and then had to face the harder question of what to build. We are on the same question, 250 years later, with worse syrup and better information. Habits can be broken without breaking people. That is the only kind of abolishment this book is for.
